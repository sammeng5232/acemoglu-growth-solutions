\begin{aex}{2.1}{}
Fix a date $t$ and write $\bar L(t)>0$ for the (finite, inelastically supplied) labor endowment and
$K(t)$ for the capital stock used in production.  In a competitive equilibrium the representative firm
solves \bk{(2.5)},
\[
\max_{K\ge0,\,L\ge0}\ F(K,L,A(t))-R(t)K-w(t)L,
\]
taking $(R(t),w(t))$ as given, and factor markets clear in the complementary slackness form \bk{(2.4)}:
\[
L(t)\le\bar L(t),\qquad w(t)\ge0,\qquad \bigl(L(t)-\bar L(t)\bigr)w(t)=0 .
\]

Suppose, to get a contradiction, that $w(t)=0$.  Let $\bigl(K(t),L(t)\bigr)$ be the equilibrium factor
demands, which must be finite since they cannot exceed the endowments.  Consider raising employment to
$L(t)+\varepsilon$ for $\varepsilon>0$, holding capital fixed.  Profits change by
\[
F\bigl(K(t),L(t)+\varepsilon,A(t)\bigr)-F\bigl(K(t),L(t),A(t)\bigr)-w(t)\varepsilon
=\int_{L(t)}^{L(t)+\varepsilon}F_L\bigl(K(t),\ell,A(t)\bigr)\dd\ell>0,
\]
because $w(t)=0$ and $F_L>0$ everywhere by \bk{Assumption 1}.  So the firm strictly prefers more labor at
\emph{every} finite $L$: the maximization problem has no solution, and in particular
$\bigl(K(t),L(t)\bigr)$ is not profit maximizing.  That contradicts firm optimization, so $w(t)>0$.

Given $w(t)>0$, the complementary slackness condition in \bk{(2.4)} forces $L(t)-\bar L(t)=0$, which is
exactly the simple market clearing condition \bk{(2.3)}, $L(t)=\bar L(t)$.  This is why \bk{(2.3)} can be
used throughout in place of \bk{(2.4)}.

\rmk{Only $F_L>0$ is used; neither constant returns nor the Inada conditions are needed.  The identical
argument with $F_K>0$ shows $R(t)>0$, so the capital market also clears with equality,
$K(t)=\bar K(t)$.  Note also that \emph{some} bound on labor demand is essential to the argument: it is the
finiteness of the endowment that turns ``the firm always wants more labor'' into a contradiction.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.2}{}
Fix $A$ and write $F(K,L)$ for $F(K,L,A)$, which by \bk{Assumption 1} is twice differentiable and
homogeneous of degree $1$ in $(K,L)$, with $F_{KK}<0$ and $F_{LL}<0$.

\emph{The Hessian is singular.}  By \bk{Theorem 2.1} (Euler's Theorem), the partial derivatives $F_K$ and
$F_L$ are homogeneous of degree $0$.  Applying Euler's Theorem to each of them,
\[
KF_{KK}+LF_{KL}=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
KF_{LK}+LF_{LL}=0 .
\]
Hence, for $K,L>0$,
\[
F_{KL}=-\frac{K}{L}F_{KK}>0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
F_{KL}=-\frac{L}{K}F_{LL}>0 ,
\]
the signs following from $F_{KK}<0$ and $F_{LL}<0$.  Multiplying the two expressions,
\[
F_{KL}^2=\Bigl(-\frac{K}{L}F_{KK}\Bigr)\Bigl(-\frac{L}{K}F_{LL}\Bigr)=F_{KK}F_{LL},
\]
so that the Hessian
\[
\mathbf{H}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{KK}&F_{KL}\\ F_{KL}&F_{LL}\end{pmatrix}
\qquad\text{has}\qquad
\det\mathbf{H}=F_{KK}F_{LL}-F_{KL}^2=0 .
\]

\emph{Concavity.}  A twice differentiable function is concave if and only if its Hessian is negative
semidefinite everywhere.  Here the leading principal minors are $F_{KK}<0$ and $\det\mathbf{H}=0$, so
$\mathbf{H}$ is negative semidefinite (its eigenvalues are $0$ and $F_{KK}+F_{LL}<0$).  Therefore $F$ is
concave in $(K,L)$.

\emph{But not strictly.}  Strict concavity would require $\mathbf{H}$ to be negative definite, which fails
since $\det\mathbf{H}=0$; the null direction is $(K,L)'$ itself, because
$\mathbf{H}\binom{K}{L}=\binom{KF_{KK}+LF_{KL}}{KF_{LK}+LF_{LL}}=\binom{0}{0}$.  The failure is easy to see
directly: along any ray through the origin, constant returns make $F$ \emph{linear},
\[
F\bigl(\lambda K+(1-\lambda)\lambda' K,\ \lambda L+(1-\lambda)\lambda' L\bigr)
=\bigl[\lambda+(1-\lambda)\lambda'\bigr]F(K,L)
=\lambda F(K,L)+(1-\lambda)F(\lambda'K,\lambda'L),
\]
so the concavity inequality holds with equality for any two points on a ray.  This is the sense in which the
production function can be ``concave but not strictly concave'' even though $F_{KK}<0$ and $F_{LL}<0$: it
curves in every direction except the direction in which the two inputs are scaled together.

\rmk{The per capita function $f(k)\equiv F(k,1)$ \emph{is} strictly concave, since
$f''(k)=F_{KK}(k,1)<0$.  Strict concavity of $f$, not of $F$, is what the uniqueness arguments of the
chapter use.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.3}{}
Write the profit function as
\[
\pi(K,L)\equiv F(K,L,A)-RK-wL .
\]
Since $F$ is homogeneous of degree $1$ in $(K,L)$, so is $\pi$: for every $\lambda>0$,
\[
\pi(\lambda K,\lambda L)=\lambda\pi(K,L).
\]
For $L>0$ write $\kappa\equiv K/L$; then constant returns give
\[
\pi(K,L)=L\bigl[f(\kappa)-R\kappa-w\bigr]\equiv L\,\varphi(\kappa),
\qquad f(\kappa)\equiv F(\kappa,1,A).
\]
Everything therefore depends on the sign of the maximized value of $\varphi$, and only three cases can
arise.

\emph{Case 1: $\varphi(\kappa)>0$ for some $\kappa$ (equivalently $\pi(K_0,L_0)>0$ for some $(K_0,L_0)$).}
Then $\pi(\lambda K_0,\lambda L_0)=\lambda\pi(K_0,L_0)\to\infty$ as $\lambda\to\infty$: profits are
unbounded and no maximizer exists.  This is the case in which factor prices are ``too low'' relative to
marginal products.

\emph{Case 2: $\varphi(\kappa)<0$ for every $\kappa\ge0$.}  Then $\pi(K,L)<0$ whenever $L>0$.  On the
boundary $L=0$ constant returns give $F(K,0,A)=K\,F(1,0,A)$, and
\[
F(1,0,A)=\lim_{L\to0}Lf(1/L)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{f(k)}{k}=0
\]
by \bk{Assumption 2}, so $\pi(K,0)=-RK\le0$ with equality only at $K=0$.  Since $\pi(0,0)=0$, the unique
maximizer is $K=L=0$ and maximal profit is zero.

\emph{Case 3: $\max_\kappa\varphi(\kappa)=0$, attained at some $\kappa^*>0$.}  Then $\pi(K,L)=0$ for every
$(K,L)$ with $K/L=\kappa^*$, and $\pi(K,L)<0$ otherwise (for $L>0$), so the solution set is the entire ray
\[
\bigl\{(K,L):K/L=\kappa^*,\ L\ge0\bigr\},
\]
a continuum: the scale of the firm is completely indeterminate, only its capital-labor ratio is pinned
down.

These are the only possibilities, since $\max_\kappa\varphi$ is either positive, negative, or zero.  In
equilibrium it is Case 3 that must obtain: factor supplies are fixed and finite, so Case 1 is inconsistent
with market clearing and Case 2 would leave the supplies unemployed.  Hence equilibrium factor prices
satisfy $\varphi(\kappa^*)=0$ with $\varphi'(\kappa^*)=f'(\kappa^*)-R=0$, which is \bk{(2.7)}, and then
$w=f(\kappa^*)-\kappa^*f'(\kappa^*)$, which is \bk{(2.6)}; profits are zero, as \bk{Proposition 2.1}
states.  The indeterminacy of scale is why the model determines only aggregates, and why the
representative firm can be taken to operate at the economy's endowment point.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.4}{}
It is convenient to factor the function once and for all:
\[
f(k)=k(k-1)(k-2)(k-3),
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\frac{f(k)}{k}=(k-1)(k-2)(k-3).
\]
Also
\[
f'(k)=4k^3-18k^2+22k-6,\qquad
f''(k)=12k^2-36k+22,
\]
\[
w(k)=f(k)-kf'(k)=-k^2\bigl(3k^2-12k+11\bigr).
\]

\emph{(a) Violations.}  The underlying production function is $F(K,L)=Lf(K/L)$, so $F_K=f'(k)$,
$F_{KK}=f''(k)/L$, $F_L=w(k)$ and $F_{LL}=(K^2/L^3)f''(k)$.  Constant returns to scale hold by
construction, and $f$ is a polynomial, hence twice (indeed infinitely) differentiable.  Everything else in
\bk{Assumption 1} fails somewhere:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Nonnegativity of output.}  $f(k)<0$ for $k\in(0,1)\cup(2,3)$ --- for instance
      $f(1/2)=-0.9375$ --- so $F$ does not map into $\R_+$.
\item \emph{Positive marginal products.}  $f'(0)=-6<0$, so $F_K>0$ fails near $k=0$ (and again on part of
      $(2,3)$).  Likewise $w(k)<0$ for $k<2-1/\sqrt3$ and for $k>2+1/\sqrt3$, \eg $w(3)=-18$, so $F_L>0$
      fails.
\item \emph{Diminishing marginal products.}  $f''(k)=12k^2-36k+22$ is positive outside the interval between
      its roots $k=(9\pm\sqrt{15})/6$, \ie on $(0,0.8545)\cup(2.1455,\infty)$ --- for instance
      $f''(0)=22>0$.  So $F_{KK}<0$ and $F_{LL}<0$ both fail there.
\end{itemize}
Of \bk{Assumption 2}, only $F(0,L,A)=0$ survives ($f(0)=0$).  Both Inada conditions for capital fail:
$\lim_{k\to0}f'(k)=-6\neq\infty$ and $f'(k)\to+\infty$ rather than $0$ as $k\to\infty$ (\eg
$f'(10)=2414$).  The Inada conditions for labor fail as well, since $F_L=w(k)\to-\infty$ as $k\to\infty$.

\emph{(b) Three steady states.}  With population growth $n$ and depreciation $\delta$, the fundamental law
of motion \bk{(2.33)} is
\[
\frac{\dt k(t)}{k(t)}=s\,\frac{f(k(t))}{k(t)}-(n+\delta),
\]
so an interior steady state is a solution of
\[
(k-1)(k-2)(k-3)=h,\qquad h\equiv\frac{n+\delta}{s}>0 .
\tag{$*$}
\]
The cubic on the left has derivative $3k^2-12k+11$, which vanishes at $k=2\mp1/\sqrt3$; it therefore rises
to a local maximum at $k=2-1/\sqrt3\approx1.42265$, falls to a local minimum at $k=2+1/\sqrt3$, and rises
thereafter.  The two extreme values are
\[
\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\Bigr)\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\Bigr)\Bigl(-1-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\Bigr)
=\frac{2}{3\sqrt3}=\frac{2\sqrt3}{9}\approx0.38490
\qquad\text{and}\qquad -\frac{2\sqrt3}{9}.
\]
Hence ($*$) has exactly three positive roots whenever
\[
0<\frac{n+\delta}{s}<\frac{2\sqrt3}{9}\approx0.38490,
\]
one in $(1,2-1/\sqrt3)$, one in $(2-1/\sqrt3,2)$, and one in $(3,\infty)$; it has exactly one (in
$(3,\infty)$) when $h>2\sqrt3/9$.  For example, with $n=0$ and $\delta/s=1/4$ the three steady states are
\[
k^*_1=1.162435,\qquad k^*_2=1.730406,\qquad k^*_3=3.107160 .
\]
Figure~\ref{fig:ch2quartic} plots the cubic together with the horizontal line $h$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/ch2_quartic_steady_states.pdf}
\caption{Steady states of the quartic economy: the interior steady states are the intersections of
$f(k)/k=(k-1)(k-2)(k-3)$ with the horizontal line $(n+\delta)/s$, drawn here at $1/4$.}

\emph{(c) Stability.}  Since $\dt k=k\,s\bigl[(k-1)(k-2)(k-3)-h\bigr]$, the sign of $\dt k$ is the sign of
the vertical distance between the cubic and the line, and at a steady state
\[
\frac{\partial\dt k}{\partial k}\Big|_{k=k^*}=s\,k^*\cdot\frac{\dd}{\dd k}\Bigl[\frac{f(k)}{k}\Bigr]_{k=k^*},
\]
so a steady state is locally stable exactly when the cubic crosses the line \emph{downward}.  The cubic
crosses upward at $k^*_1$ (rising branch), downward at $k^*_2$ (falling branch), and upward again at
$k^*_3$:
\[
\text{at }k^*_1:\ +1.1045,\qquad
\text{at }k^*_2:\ -0.7820,\qquad
\text{at }k^*_3:\ +2.6774 ,
\]
for the derivative of $f(k)/k$.  Therefore $k^*_2$ is locally asymptotically stable while $k^*_1$ and
$k^*_3$ are unstable.  In addition $k=0$ is a rest point of the differential equation, and it is locally
stable: for small $k>0$ we have $f(k)/k<0<h$, so $\dt k<0$ and the economy collapses to zero.  The complete
phase line is
\[
\underbrace{0}_{\text{stable}}\ \longleftarrow\ \underbrace{k^*_1}_{\text{unstable}}\ \longrightarrow\
\underbrace{k^*_2}_{\text{stable}}\ \longleftarrow\ \underbrace{k^*_3}_{\text{unstable}}\ \longrightarrow\
\infty .
\]
An economy starting with $k(0)<k^*_1$ collapses to $k=0$; one starting in $(k^*_1,k^*_3)$ converges to
$k^*_2$; one starting above $k^*_3$ grows without bound.  This is a poverty-trap configuration: history
(the initial condition) decides the long-run outcome.

Finally, \emph{no} steady state can be globally stable.  Global asymptotic stability requires convergence
to the same point from every initial condition; but an economy that starts at another steady state stays
there forever, so the existence of more than one rest point rules global stability out immediately.

\rmk{Part (c) of the exercise asks one to prove that \emph{two} of the three steady states in (b) are
locally stable.  Of the three \emph{interior} steady states exactly one --- the middle one --- is locally
stable, as the sign pattern above shows: on the rising branches of $f(k)/k$ the economy is pushed away from
the steady state.  The count in the book is recovered only if the degenerate rest point $k=0$, which the
text elsewhere excludes as having ``no economic content'', is included among the steady states; then two of
the four rest points ($k=0$ and $k^*_2$) are stable and two are unstable.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.5}{}
We must show that under \bk{Assumptions 1} and \bk{2} the continuous-time Solow model with population
growth at the rate $n$ has a unique steady-state equilibrium, with $k^*\in(0,\infty)$ solving \bk{(2.34)},
$y^*=f(k^*)$ and $c^*=(1-s)f(k^*)$.

By \bk{(2.33)} the equilibrium path satisfies $\dt k(t)/k(t)=sf(k(t))/k(t)-(n+\delta)$, so a steady state
with $k>0$ is precisely a solution of
\[
\psi(k)\equiv\frac{f(k)}{k}=\frac{n+\delta}{s} .
\tag{$*$}
\]

\emph{$\psi$ is continuous and strictly decreasing.}  Continuity is immediate from \bk{Assumption 1}.  For
monotonicity, differentiate:
\[
\psi'(k)=\frac{f'(k)k-f(k)}{k^2}=-\frac{w(k)}{k^2},
\qquad w(k)=f(k)-kf'(k) .
\]
Since $f$ is strictly concave with $f(0)=0$ (\bk{Assumption 2}), strict concavity gives
$f(k)>f(0)+kf'(k)=kf'(k)$, that is $w(k)>0$ for every $k>0$; equivalently, $w(k)=F_L>0$ is the wage, which
is positive by \bk{Assumption 1}.  Hence $\psi'(k)<0$ for all $k>0$.

\emph{The limits.}  Both $f(k)\to0$ and $k\to0$ as $k\to0$, so l'H\^opital's rule and \bk{Assumption 2}
give
\[
\lim_{k\to0}\psi(k)=\lim_{k\to0}f'(k)=\lim_{K\to0}F_K=\infty ,
\qquad
\lim_{k\to\infty}\psi(k)=\lim_{k\to\infty}f'(k)=\lim_{K\to\infty}F_K=0 .
\]
(The second limit also follows from $0<f(k)/k$ and concavity.)

\emph{Existence and uniqueness.}  $\psi$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$ with limits $\infty$ at $0$ and $0$
at $\infty$, so by the Intermediate Value Theorem it attains the value $(n+\delta)/s>0$ at some
$k^*\in(0,\infty)$; since $\psi$ is strictly decreasing, that value is attained exactly once.  Hence there
is a unique steady-state capital-labor ratio, given by \bk{(2.34)}.

Finally, at $k^*$ the level of output per capita is $y^*=f(k^*)$ by definition of $f$, and since a constant
fraction $s$ of income is saved, consumption per capita is $c^*=(1-s)f(k^*)$, with the corresponding
investment $sf(k^*)=(n+\delta)k^*$ exactly replacing depreciated capital and equipping the new workers.

\rmk{The proof is that of \bk{Proposition 2.2} with $\delta$ replaced by $n+\delta$: going from discrete to
continuous time, and adding population growth, changes nothing essential.  Population growth acts exactly
like faster depreciation, since both erode the capital-labor ratio at a constant proportional rate.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.6}{}
Let $f(k)=A\wt f(k)$ with $\wt f$ satisfying \bk{Assumptions 1} and \bk{2}, and let $k^*$ be the unique
steady state of Exercise 2.5, \ie the solution of
\[
G(k^*;A,s,\delta,n)\equiv sA\,\frac{\wt f(k^*)}{k^*}-(n+\delta)=0 .
\]
Writing $\wt w(k)\equiv\wt f(k)-k\wt f'(k)>0$ for the wage per unit of $A$, the computation in Exercise 2.5
gives
\[
\frac{\partial G}{\partial k^*}=sA\cdot\frac{\dd}{\dd k}\Bigl[\frac{\wt f(k)}{k}\Bigr]_{k=k^*}
=-\frac{sA\,\wt w(k^*)}{(k^*)^2}<0 ,
\]
so the Implicit Function Theorem applies at every parameter configuration and yields
$\partial k^*/\partial x=-(\partial G/\partial x)\big/(\partial G/\partial k^*)$ for each parameter $x$.
Since $\partial G/\partial A=s\wt f(k^*)/k^*>0$, $\partial G/\partial s=A\wt f(k^*)/k^*>0$,
$\partial G/\partial\delta=-1$ and $\partial G/\partial n=-1$,
\[
\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial A}=\frac{k^*\wt f(k^*)}{A\,\wt w(k^*)}>0,\qquad
\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial s}=\frac{k^*\wt f(k^*)}{s\,\wt w(k^*)}>0,\qquad
\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial\delta}=\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial n}
=-\frac{(k^*)^2}{sA\,\wt w(k^*)}<0 .
\]
This proves the four statements about $k^*$.

For output, $y^*=A\wt f(k^*)$, so for $x\in\{s,\delta,n\}$ the chain rule gives
\[
\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial x}=A\wt f'(k^*)\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial x},
\]
which has the same sign as $\partial k^*/\partial x$ because $\wt f'>0$: $\partial y^*/\partial s>0$ and
$\partial y^*/\partial\delta,\ \partial y^*/\partial n<0$.  For $A$ there is in addition a direct effect,
and both effects push the same way:
\[
\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial A}=\wt f(k^*)+A\wt f'(k^*)\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial A}>0 .
\]

The economics is transparent.  A higher saving rate or a better technology raises investment at every
capital-labor ratio and so shifts the $sf(k)/k$ schedule up, moving its intersection with the horizontal
line $n+\delta$ to the right.  A higher depreciation rate or a higher rate of population growth raises the
amount of investment needed merely to hold $k$ constant --- capital must be replaced, and new workers must
be equipped --- and so moves the intersection to the left.  The only new result relative to
\bk{Proposition 2.3} is the effect of $n$, and it enters exactly as $\delta$ does: countries with faster
population growth are, other things equal, poorer in per capita terms.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.8}{}
Throughout, $F$ is twice differentiable and exhibits constant returns to scale, $f(k)=F(k,1,A)$ is concave
(so $f''\le0$) with $f(0)=0$, and the Inada conditions of \bk{Assumption 2} hold.  What is \emph{not}
assumed is $F_{KK}<0$, $F_{LL}<0$, or even $F_K>0$ and $F_L>0$.  The first thing to notice is that the
missing sign restrictions on the first derivatives come for free.

\emph{Step 1: the marginal product of capital is nonnegative.}  Concavity makes $f'$ nonincreasing.  If
$f'(k_0)<0$ for some $k_0$, then $f'(k)\le f'(k_0)<0$ for every $k\ge k_0$, contradicting the Inada
condition $\lim_{k\to\infty}f'(k)=0$.  Hence $f'(k)\ge0$, that is $F_K\ge0$, for all $k>0$.  (One cannot
get more: a technology that is affine from some $k_0$ on has $f'\equiv0$ beyond $k_0$ and still satisfies
every hypothesis.  As the rest of the argument shows, $f'\ge0$ is all that is needed.)

\emph{Step 2: the wage is strictly positive.}  Write $w(k)=f(k)-kf'(k)$.  Since $f(0)=0$,
\[
w(k)=\int_0^k\bigl[f'(x)-f'(k)\bigr]\dd x ,
\]
and the integrand is nonnegative because $f'$ is nonincreasing.  It is moreover strictly positive on a set
of positive measure: $f'(x)\to\infty$ as $x\to0$ while $f'(k)<\infty$, so $f'(x)>f'(k)$ for all small $x$.
Hence $w(k)>0$ for every $k>0$; that is, $F_L>0$, and the ``not necessarily strictly'' concave technology
still pays a positive wage.

\emph{\bk{Proposition 2.2}: existence and uniqueness of the steady state.}  Exactly as in Exercise 2.5,
$\psi(k)\equiv f(k)/k$ satisfies $\psi'(k)=-w(k)/k^2<0$ by Step 2, so $\psi$ is \emph{strictly} decreasing
even though $f$ is only weakly concave.  Its limits are $\infty$ at $0$ and $0$ at $\infty$ by
\bk{Assumption 2} and l'H\^opital's rule.  The Intermediate Value Theorem therefore gives a unique
$k^*\in(0,\infty)$ with $f(k^*)/k^*=\delta/s$, and then $y^*=f(k^*)$ and $c^*=(1-s)f(k^*)$.  Nothing in the
argument used strict concavity; it used only that $w>0$, which Step 2 delivers.

\emph{\bk{Proposition 2.5}: global stability.}  Let $g(k)\equiv sf(k)+(1-\delta)k$, so that the discrete
time law of motion is $k(t+1)=g(k(t))$ and $k^*=g(k^*)$.  By Step 1, $g'(k)=sf'(k)+1-\delta>0$.  By Step 2,
$\delta=sf(k^*)/k^*>sf'(k^*)$, whence
\[
g'(k^*)=sf'(k^*)+1-\delta\in(0,1),
\]
and local stability follows from \bk{Corollary 2.1}.  For global stability, take $k(t)\in(0,k^*)$.  Since
$g$ is strictly increasing, $k(t+1)=g(k(t))<g(k^*)=k^*$; and since $\psi$ is strictly decreasing,
\[
\frac{k(t+1)-k(t)}{k(t)}=s\frac{f(k(t))}{k(t)}-\delta>s\frac{f(k^*)}{k^*}-\delta=0 .
\]
So $k(t+1)\in(k(t),k^*)$: the sequence is increasing and bounded above by $k^*$, hence convergent, and its
limit is a fixed point of $g$ in $(0,k^*]$, which can only be $k^*$ by uniqueness.  The argument starting
from $k(0)>k^*$ is identical with the inequalities reversed.  Monotone global convergence therefore
survives the weakening of \bk{Assumption 1}.

\emph{\bk{Proposition 2.6} must be weakened to weak monotonicity.}  The proposition asserts that
$\{w(t)\}$ is an increasing and $\{R(t)\}$ a decreasing sequence when $k(0)<k^*$.  With $f''\le0$ instead of
$f''<0$ we get
\[
R'(k)=f''(k)\le0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
w'(k)=-kf''(k)\ge0 ,
\]
so the correct statement is that $\{w(t)\}$ is \emph{nondecreasing} and $\{R(t)\}$ is \emph{nonincreasing}
(with the inequalities reversed when $k(0)>k^*$).  Both can be locally constant: on any interval where $f$
is affine --- the production function is then locally an $AK$ technology --- the capital-labor ratio keeps
rising while factor prices do not move at all.  Strict monotonicity is restored as soon as $f''<0$ on the
relevant range, which is what \bk{Assumption 1} guarantees.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.9}{}
Let \bk{Assumptions 1} and \bk{2} hold and suppose $k(0)<k^*$.  By \bk{Proposition 2.5} the capital-labor
ratio increases monotonically to $k^*$: $k(t+1)>k(t)$ for every $t$, with $k(t)\to k^*$.

In equilibrium factor prices are the marginal products, and with constant returns they depend on the
capital-labor ratio alone:
\[
R(t)=F_K\bigl(K(t),L(t),A\bigr)=f'\bigl(k(t)\bigr),
\]
\[
w(t)=F_L\bigl(K(t),L(t),A\bigr)=f\bigl(k(t)\bigr)-k(t)f'\bigl(k(t)\bigr)=w\bigl(k(t)\bigr).
\]
Differentiating the two expressions in $k$,
\[
\frac{\dd R}{\dd k}=f''(k)<0,
\qquad
\frac{\dd w}{\dd k}=f'(k)-f'(k)-kf''(k)=-kf''(k)>0 ,
\]
both strict by \bk{Assumption 1}, which has $f''(k)=F_{KK}(k,1,A)<0$.

So $R(\cdot)$ is a strictly decreasing and $w(\cdot)$ a strictly increasing function of $k$.  Composing
with the strictly increasing sequence $\{k(t)\}$ gives
\[
w(t+1)=w\bigl(k(t+1)\bigr)>w\bigl(k(t)\bigr)=w(t),
\qquad
R(t+1)=f'\bigl(k(t+1)\bigr)<f'\bigl(k(t)\bigr)=R(t),
\]
that is, $\{w(t)\}_{t=0}^\infty$ is an increasing and $\{R(t)\}_{t=0}^\infty$ a decreasing sequence, and
they converge to $w(k^*)$ and $f'(k^*)$.  If instead $k(0)>k^*$, \bk{Proposition 2.5} gives a strictly
decreasing $\{k(t)\}$ and the two conclusions are reversed.

The economics is the usual scarcity argument: capital deepening makes capital abundant relative to labor,
so the marginal product of capital --- and with it the rental rate and the interest rate $r=R-\delta$ ---
falls, while the marginal product of labor, and hence the wage, rises.  Along the transition the entire
increase in output per worker that is not saved accrues to labor.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.10}{}
\emph{Part 1.}  The linear equation $\dt x(t)=ax(t)$ with $x(0)$ given has the unique solution
$x(t)=x(0)\exp(at)$.  If $a<0$ then $\exp(at)\to0$, so $x(t)\to0$ for every initial condition: the steady
state $x^*=0$ is globally asymptotically stable.  (If $a>0$ the solution diverges unless $x(0)=0$, and if
$a=0$ every point is a steady state.)

\emph{Part 2.}  Let $g$ be differentiable in a neighborhood of $x^*$, with $g(x^*)=0$ and $g'(x^*)<0$.  This
is the scalar case ($n=1$) of \bk{Theorem 2.5}: the Jacobian is the number $g'(x^*)$, its unique eigenvalue
is $g'(x^*)<0$, which has negative real part, so $x^*$ is locally asymptotically stable.  The direct
argument is just as short.  Since $g$ is continuous with $g(x^*)=0$ and $g'(x^*)<0$, there is
$\varepsilon>0$ with
\[
g(x)<0\ \text{ for }x\in(x^*,x^*+\varepsilon)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
g(x)>0\ \text{ for }x\in(x^*-\varepsilon,x^*),
\]
so on that neighborhood $\dt x$ always points toward $x^*$; Part 3 applied to the interval
$(x^*-\varepsilon,x^*+\varepsilon)$ then gives convergence.

\emph{Part 3.}  Let $g$ be continuously differentiable with $g(x^*)=0$, $g(x)<0$ for $x>x^*$ and $g(x)>0$
for $x<x^*$.  Take any $x(0)<x^*$ (the case $x(0)>x^*$ is symmetric, and $x(0)=x^*$ is trivial).

Because $g$ is continuously differentiable it is locally Lipschitz, so by the Picard--Lindel\"of theorem
the solution through any point is unique.  The constant function $x\equiv x^*$ solves the equation, so the
trajectory starting below $x^*$ can never reach $x^*$ in finite time, and \emph{a fortiori} never crosses
it: $x(t)<x^*$ for all $t$.  Consequently $g(x(t))>0$ and $\dt x(t)>0$ for all $t$, so $x(\cdot)$ is
strictly increasing and bounded above by $x^*$.  A monotone bounded function converges; let
$L\equiv\lim_{t\to\infty}x(t)\le x^*$.

Suppose $L<x^*$.  By continuity $\dt x(t)=g(x(t))\to g(L)>0$, so there are $T$ and $\eta>0$ with
$\dt x(t)\ge\eta$ for all $t\ge T$, whence $x(t)\ge x(T)+\eta(t-T)\to\infty$, contradicting $x(t)\le x^*$.
Therefore $L=x^*$, and $x(t)\to x^*$ from every initial condition: $x^*$ is globally asymptotically stable.

\rmk{Part 3 is what makes the continuous-time Solow model so much better behaved than its discrete-time
counterpart.  In discrete time a one-dimensional map can jump over its fixed point, so a sign condition on
$g$ alone does not preclude two-cycles; \bk{Exercise 2.21} constructs exactly such a cycle.  In continuous
time trajectories are continuous and cannot cross a rest point, which is why the analogue of Part 3 fails
in discrete time.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.13}{}
\emph{The law of motion.}  Labor income per worker is the wage $w(k)=f(k)-kf'(k)$, of which a fraction $s$
is saved; capital income $f'(k)k$ is entirely consumed.  Aggregate saving is therefore $sw(k(t))L(t)$, and
with population growth at the rate $n$ and depreciation $\delta$ the capital-labor ratio obeys
\[
\dt k(t)=s\,w\bigl(k(t)\bigr)-(n+\delta)k(t),
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\frac{\dt k(t)}{k(t)}=s\,\phi\bigl(k(t)\bigr)-(n+\delta),
\qquad \phi(k)\equiv\frac{w(k)}{k}.
\]
Interior steady states are the solutions of
\[
\phi(k)=\frac{w(k)}{k}=\frac{n+\delta}{s} .
\tag{$*$}
\]
The contrast with the baseline model is exactly the replacement of $f(k)/k$ by $w(k)/k$.  In the baseline
model $f(k)/k$ is strictly decreasing, which is what delivered uniqueness in Exercise 2.5.  Nothing of the
sort holds for $w(k)/k$.

\emph{When is $\phi$ increasing?}  Differentiating,
\[
\phi'(k)=\frac{kw'(k)-w(k)}{k^2}
=\frac{-k^2f''(k)-w(k)}{k^2},
\]
using $w'(k)=-kf''(k)$.  Now recall that for a constant returns technology the elasticity of substitution
\bk{(2.37)} can be written as
\[
\sigma(k)=\frac{f'(k)\,w(k)}{-k\,f(k)\,f''(k)} ,
\]
so that $-k^2f''(k)=\alpha_K(k)\,w(k)/\sigma(k)$ with $\alpha_K(k)\equiv kf'(k)/f(k)$ the share of capital
in national income.  Substituting,
\[
\phi'(k)=\frac{w(k)}{k^2}\Bigl[\frac{\alpha_K(k)}{\sigma(k)}-1\Bigr],
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\phi'(k)>0\iff\sigma(k)<\alpha_K(k).
\]
So $w(k)/k$ rises precisely where capital and labor are hard to substitute relative to the capital share.
Intuitively, when $\sigma$ is small an increase in capital raises the wage more than proportionately,
because labor becomes the scarce complementary factor; saving out of wages then feeds back into more
capital, and the economy can have several rest points.

\emph{An explicit example.}  Let
\[
f(k)=A\bigl[\gamma k^{\rho}+(1-\gamma)\bigr]^{1/\rho}+Bk^{\alpha},
\qquad \rho=\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}<0,\quad\alpha\in(0,1),\ B>0 :
\]
a CES technology with a low elasticity of substitution plus a small Cobb--Douglas term.  Both terms are
increasing, strictly concave and homogeneous of degree one in $(K,L)$ when written as $Lf(K/L)$, so
\bk{Assumption 1} holds.  The CES term alone would violate \bk{Assumption 2}, since
$f'_{\text{CES}}(0)=A\gamma^{1/\rho}<\infty$ and $w_{\text{CES}}(\infty)=A(1-\gamma)^{1/\rho}<\infty$; the
Cobb--Douglas term repairs both, because $Bk^{\alpha}$ contributes $B\alpha k^{\alpha-1}\to\infty$ to
$f'(k)$ as $k\to0$ and $(1-\alpha)Bk^{\alpha}\to\infty$ to $w(k)$ as $k\to\infty$.  So $f$ satisfies
\bk{Assumptions 1} and \bk{2} in full, and
\[
w(k)=A(1-\gamma)\bigl[\gamma k^{\rho}+(1-\gamma)\bigr]^{(1-\rho)/\rho}+(1-\alpha)Bk^{\alpha} .
\]
Take $A=1$, $\gamma=1/2$, $\sigma=1/4$ (so $\rho=-3$), $B=0.02$ and $\alpha=1/2$.  Then $\phi(k)=w(k)/k$
falls from $+\infty$ to a local minimum $\phi=0.03005$ at $k=0.1509$, rises to a local maximum
$\phi=0.60360$ at $k=1.4340$, and falls back to $0$; the turning points are exactly where $\sigma(k)$
crosses $\alpha_K(k)$:
\[
\begin{array}{lcccc}
k & 0.05 & 0.30 & 1.00 & 3.00\\
\sigma(k) & 1.8871 & 0.3581 & 0.2537 & 0.3264\\
\alpha_K(k) & 0.9667 & 0.9603 & 0.5000 & 0.0483\\
\phi'(k) & -0.438 & +0.286 & +0.495 & -0.115
\end{array}
\]
Consequently ($*$) has \emph{three} solutions for every $(n+\delta)/s$ strictly between $0.03005$ and
$0.60360$.  With $(n+\delta)/s=0.045$ they are
\[
k^*_1=0.04972,\qquad k^*_2=0.27684,\qquad k^*_3=29.19752 ,
\]
and since $\dt k$ has the sign of $\phi(k)-(n+\delta)/s$, the outer two are locally asymptotically stable
($\phi'(k^*_1)=-0.442$, $\phi'(k^*_3)=-0.002$) while the middle one is unstable ($\phi'(k^*_2)=+0.240$).
Figure~\ref{fig:ch2wage} plots $\phi$ and the horizontal line.  The economy converges to the poor or to the
rich steady state according to whether it starts below or above $k^*_2$: with saving out of wages alone,
the Solow model produces a poverty trap.

\rmk{The comparison with \bk{Exercise 2.12} is instructive.  When only capital income is saved, the law of
motion is $\dt k/k=s_Kf'(k)-(n+\delta)$, and $f'$ is strictly decreasing by \bk{Assumption 1}: the steady
state is unique and globally stable, whatever the elasticity of substitution.  It is the wage --- the
factor price that \emph{rises} with capital --- that can generate the self-reinforcing accumulation behind

\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.15}{}
With constant returns, output per worker and the wage are both functions of the capital-labor ratio alone:
\[
y=f(k),\qquad w=F_L=f(k)-kf'(k),
\]
the second equality being the competitive wage.  Along the family of economies indexed by $k$,
\[
\frac{\dd y}{\dd k}=f'(k),
\qquad
\frac{\dd w}{\dd k}=f'(k)-f'(k)-kf''(k)=-kf''(k)>0 ,
\]
so $w$ is a strictly increasing function of $k$ and can be inverted; by the chain rule,
\[
\frac{\partial y}{\partial w}=\frac{\dd y/\dd k}{\dd w/\dd k}=\frac{f'(k)}{-kf''(k)} .
\]
Therefore
\[
\varepsilon_{y,w}=\frac{\partial y}{\partial w}\cdot\frac{w}{y}
=\frac{f'(k)}{-kf''(k)}\cdot\frac{f(k)-kf'(k)}{f(k)}
=\frac{f'(k)\bigl[f(k)-kf'(k)\bigr]}{-k\,f(k)\,f''(k)} .
\tag{$*$}
\]

It remains to check that the right-hand side of ($*$) is the elasticity of substitution \bk{(2.37)}.  With
constant returns, $F_K=f'(k)$ and $F_L=f(k)-kf'(k)$, so
\[
\frac{\partial\log(F_K/F_L)}{\partial\log k}
=k\frac{\dd}{\dd k}\Bigl[\log f'(k)-\log\bigl(f(k)-kf'(k)\bigr)\Bigr]
=k\Bigl[\frac{f''(k)}{f'(k)}+\frac{kf''(k)}{f(k)-kf'(k)}\Bigr] .
\]
Putting the bracket over a common denominator,
\[
\frac{\partial\log(F_K/F_L)}{\partial\log k}
=k f''(k)\,\frac{\bigl[f(k)-kf'(k)\bigr]+kf'(k)}{f'(k)\bigl[f(k)-kf'(k)\bigr]}
=\frac{k\,f(k)\,f''(k)}{f'(k)\bigl[f(k)-kf'(k)\bigr]} ,
\]
and hence, by the definition \bk{(2.37)},
\[
\sigma=-\Bigl[\frac{\partial\log(F_K/F_L)}{\partial\log(K/L)}\Bigr]^{-1}
=\frac{f'(k)\bigl[f(k)-kf'(k)\bigr]}{-k\,f(k)\,f''(k)} ,
\]
which is exactly ($*$).  Therefore $\varepsilon_{y,w}=\sigma$.

The result is the basis of the empirical strategy of Arrow et al.\ (1961) taken up in
\bk{Exercise 2.16}: regressing the logarithm of output per capita on the logarithm of the wage across
economies (or across time) estimates the elasticity of substitution directly, and a slope of $1$ --- which
is what those authors found to be a good approximation --- says that the technology is Cobb--Douglas.  It
also gives a clean reading of $\sigma$: it measures how much richer, in output per worker, an economy must
be in order to sustain a given proportional increase in its wage.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.24}{}
Normalize the technology terms to $A_H=A_K=A_L=1$ (they only rescale the arguments and the level, and none

\]

\]

\emph{Case $\sigma=1$.}  Then $f(k)=k^{\gamma}$ with $\gamma\in(0,1)$: $f(0)=0$,

$w(k)=(1-\gamma)k^{\gamma}\to0$ as $k\to0$ and $\to\infty$ as $k\to\infty$.  All the requirements of
\bk{Assumption 2} hold.

economically meaningful rather than technical: with $\sigma>1$ the two factors are good enough substitutes
that capital alone can produce, and accumulating it never drives its marginal product to zero; with
$\sigma<1$ they are such poor substitutes that the first units of capital are not infinitely productive,
and an economy with a fixed labor force cannot make the wage arbitrarily large.

\rmk{\bk{Assumption 1}, by contrast, holds for every finite $\sigma>0$.  Logarithmic differentiation of
$f'$ gives
\[
\frac{f''(k)}{f'(k)}=\frac{\rho-1}{k}+\frac{(1-\rho)\gamma k^{\rho-1}}{\gamma k^{\rho}+(1-\gamma)}
=-\frac{(1-\rho)(1-\gamma)}{k\bigl[\gamma k^{\rho}+(1-\gamma)\bigr]}<0
\qquad\text{for all }\rho<1 ,
\]
so $f''<0$, and $F_{KK},F_{LL}<0$ follow.  The text on page~55 states instead that ``the CES production
function with $\sigma>1$ violates Assumption 1''; what the computation above shows, and what this exercise
asks one to prove, is that what fails is \bk{Assumption 2}.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.25}{}
With Harrod-neutral technological progress at the rate $g$ and population growth at the rate $n$, the
effective capital-labor ratio $k(t)=K(t)/\bigl(A(t)L(t)\bigr)$ obeys \bk{(2.51)}, and the BGP value $k^*$
solves \bk{(2.52)},
\[
H(k^*;s,\delta,n,g)\equiv s\,\frac{f(k^*)}{k^*}-(\delta+g+n)=0 .
\]

\emph{The level of technology does not enter.}  $A(0)$ appears nowhere in this equation --- it has been
normalized away by the definition of $k$ --- so
\[
\frac{\partial k^*\bigl(A(0),s,\delta,n,g\bigr)}{\partial A(0)}=0 .
\]

\emph{The other comparative statics.}  Exactly as in Exercise 2.5,
\[
\frac{\partial H}{\partial k^*}=s\cdot\frac{\dd}{\dd k}\Bigl[\frac{f(k)}{k}\Bigr]_{k=k^*}
=-\frac{s\,w(k^*)}{(k^*)^2}<0,
\]
where $w(k)=f(k)-kf'(k)>0$.  The Implicit Function Theorem applies and gives, with
$\partial H/\partial s=f(k^*)/k^*>0$ and $\partial H/\partial\delta=\partial H/\partial n=
\partial H/\partial g=-1$,
\[
\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial s}=\frac{k^*f(k^*)}{s\,w(k^*)}>0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial n}=\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial\delta}=\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial g}
=-\frac{(k^*)^2}{s\,w(k^*)}<0 .
\]

\emph{Output per capita.}  Along the BGP, income per capita is $y^*(t)=A(t)f(k^*)=A(0)e^{gt}f(k^*)$ by
\bk{(2.50)}.  Hence, for each $t$,
\[
\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial A(0)}=e^{gt}f(k^*)>0 ,
\]
which is the one comparative static that is new relative to \bk{Proposition 2.8}: a higher initial
technology level leaves the effective capital-labor ratio untouched but raises income per capita
proportionately, at every date.  For the remaining parameters the effect runs through $k^*$ alone,
\[
\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial x}=A(0)e^{gt}f'(k^*)\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial x},
\qquad x\in\{s,\delta,n\} ,
\]
which is positive for $s$ and negative for $\delta$ and $n$, since $f'>0$.

\rmk{The proposition lists no comparative static with respect to $g$, and for good reason: a higher $g$
lowers $k^*$ and hence the level of income per capita at any fixed date, but it raises its growth rate, so
$\partial y^*(t)/\partial g$ changes sign as $t$ grows.  This is the first appearance of a theme that
recurs throughout the book --- level effects and growth effects need not point the same way.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{2.26}{}
Let \bk{Assumptions 1} and \bk{2} hold and consider the law of motion \bk{(2.51)} of the effective
capital-labor ratio,
\[
\dt k(t)=G\bigl(k(t)\bigr),
\qquad
G(k)\equiv sf(k)-(\delta+g+n)k .
\]
$G$ is continuously differentiable on $(0,\infty)$ by \bk{Assumption 1}, and by \bk{Proposition 2.11} it
has a unique zero $k^*\in(0,\infty)$, characterized by $f(k^*)/k^*=(\delta+g+n)/s$.

Write $G(k)=k\bigl[s\psi(k)-(\delta+g+n)\bigr]$ with $\psi(k)\equiv f(k)/k$.  As established in
Exercise 2.5, $\psi$ is strictly decreasing, with $\psi(k)\to\infty$ as $k\to0$ and $\psi(k)\to0$ as
$k\to\infty$.  Therefore
\[
k<k^*\implies\psi(k)>\psi(k^*)=\frac{\delta+g+n}{s}\implies G(k)>0 ,
\]
\[
k>k^*\implies\psi(k)<\psi(k^*)\implies G(k)<0 .
\]
That is exactly the hypothesis of part 3 of \bk{Corollary 2.2} (proved in Exercise 2.10), which therefore
gives global asymptotic stability: starting from any $k(0)>0$, $k(t)\to k^*$.  Moreover the convergence is
monotone, since $\dt k$ keeps a constant sign along the path: an economy that starts below its BGP
accumulates effective capital throughout the transition, and one that starts above decumulates it.

Local stability can also be read off the derivative at the rest point.  Using $sf(k^*)=(\delta+g+n)k^*$,
\[
G'(k^*)=sf'(k^*)-(\delta+g+n)=sf'(k^*)-\frac{sf(k^*)}{k^*}=-\frac{s\,w(k^*)}{k^*}<0 ,
\]
because the wage $w(k^*)=f(k^*)-k^*f'(k^*)$ is strictly positive.  So the linearization has a strictly
negative eigenvalue, confirming local asymptotic stability by part 2 of \bk{Corollary 2.2}, and giving in
addition the speed of convergence: near the BGP the gap $k(t)-k^*$ closes at the exponential rate
$s\,w(k^*)/k^*=(\delta+g+n)\,\alpha_L(k^*)$, where $\alpha_L=w/f$ is the share of labor in national income.

Since $k(t)\to k^*$ and $y(t)=A(t)f(k(t))$, output per capita converges to the balanced growth path
$A(0)e^{gt}f(k^*)$ and grows asymptotically at the rate $g$: with Harrod-neutral progress, transitional
dynamics and long-run growth separate cleanly, and the model behaves exactly as it did without
technological change once variables are measured per efficiency unit of labor.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{3.3}{}
Write the technology in labor-augmenting form, $Y=K^{\alpha}(AL)^{1-\alpha}$, so that in terms of the
effective capital-labor ratio $k=K/(AL)$,
\[
\hat y=k^{\alpha},\qquad y=Ak^{\alpha},\qquad
\frac{\dt k}{k}=sk^{\alpha-1}-(\delta+g+n),\qquad
k^*=\Bigl(\frac{s}{\delta+g+n}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} .
\]

\emph{The exact equation.}  Differentiating $y=Ak^{\alpha}$ logarithmically and substituting the law of
motion, with no approximation at all,
\[
\frac{\dt y}{y}=g+\alpha\frac{\dt k}{k}
=g+\alpha\bigl[sk^{\alpha-1}-(\delta+g+n)\bigr]
=g+\alpha(\delta+g+n)\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{k}{k^*}\Bigr)^{\alpha-1}-1\Bigr],
\]
the last step using $s(k^*)^{\alpha-1}=\delta+g+n$.  Since $y/y^*=(k/k^*)^{\alpha}$, we have
$\log k-\log k^*=\alpha^{-1}(\log y-\log y^*)$, and therefore
\[
\boxed{\ \frac{\dt y(t)}{y(t)}
=g+\alpha(\delta+g+n)\Bigl[\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\bigl(\log y(t)-\log y^*(t)\bigr)\Bigr)-1\Bigr]\ }
\tag{$*$}
\]
which is the exact analogue of \bk{(3.10)}.  Expanding the exponential to first order around
$\log y=\log y^*$ returns $\dt y/y\approx g-(1-\alpha)(\delta+g+n)(\log y-\log y^*)$, which is
\bk{(3.11)}: the book's equation is the linearization of ($*$).

\emph{The rate of convergence is not a constant.}  Write $z\equiv\log y(t)-\log y^*(t)$ for the gap and
define the convergence coefficient implicitly by $\dt y/y=g-\beta(z)\,z$, so that from ($*$)
\[
\beta(z)=\alpha(\delta+g+n)\,\frac{1-e^{-cz}}{z},
\qquad c\equiv\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}>0 .
\]
The function $\varphi(u)\equiv(1-e^{-u})/u$ has $\varphi'(u)=e^{-u}\bigl[u+1-e^{u}\bigr]/u^{2}<0$ for
$u\neq0$, because $e^{u}>1+u$; and $\varphi(u)\to1$ as $u\to0$.  Hence
\[
\beta \text{ is strictly decreasing in } z,
\qquad
\beta(0)=\alpha(\delta+g+n)\,c=(1-\alpha)(\delta+g+n),
\]
\[
\beta(z)\to\infty\ \text{ as } z\to-\infty,
\qquad
\beta(z)\to0\ \text{ as } z\to+\infty .
\]
So for a country \emph{below} its steady state ($z<0$, the empirically relevant case) the true speed of
convergence \emph{exceeds} the constant $(1-\alpha)(\delta+g+n)$ of the linearized equation, and it does so
by more the poorer the country is; for a country above its steady state convergence is slower than the
linear approximation says.  With the parameter values of \bk{Example 3.1} ($\alpha=1/3$, $\delta+g+n=0.08$,
so $(1-\alpha)(\delta+g+n)=0.0533$):

\begin{aex}{3.4}{}
\emph{Log-linear answer.}  By \bk{(3.11)}, each country's gap $z_i(t)\equiv\log y_i(t)-\log y^*(t)$ obeys
$\dt z_i=-\beta z_i$ with $\beta=(1-\alpha)(\delta+g+n)$.  Since the two countries share $y^*(t)$, the
\emph{relative} gap $D(t)\equiv\log y_1(t)-\log y_2(t)=z_1(t)-z_2(t)$ obeys the same linear equation,
\[
\dt D=-\beta D
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
D(t)=D(0)e^{-\beta t} .
\]
With $\alpha=1/3$, $g=0.02$, $n=0.01$ and $\delta=0.05$ we get $\beta=\tfrac23\times0.08=0.0533$ per year.
Starting from $D(0)=\log2=0.6931$ and requiring $D(T)=\log1.1=0.0953$,
\[
T=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\frac{\log 2}{\log 1.1}
=\frac{\log(7.273)}{0.0533}
=\frac{1.9843}{0.0533}
\approx 37.2\ \text{years} .
\]
The half-life of the gap is $\log2/\beta=13.0$ years, which is the ``little more than 10 years'' quoted in
\bk{Example 3.1}.

\emph{Exact answer.}  The Cobb--Douglas model can be solved in closed form, so the approximation can be
checked.  With $x\equiv k^{1-\alpha}$ the law of motion is linear,
$\dt x=(1-\alpha)\bigl[s-(\delta+g+n)x\bigr]$, so
\[
\frac{x(t)}{x^*}=1+\Bigl(\frac{x(0)}{x^*}-1\Bigr)e^{-\beta t},
\qquad
\frac{y(t)}{y^*(t)}=\Bigl(\frac{x(t)}{x^*}\Bigr)^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}} ,
\]
with the \emph{same} $\beta=(1-\alpha)(\delta+g+n)$ now appearing exactly.  The log gap between the two
countries is no longer exactly exponential, because the map from $x$ to $y$ is concave, so the answer
depends on where the pair sits relative to the common steady state:
\[
\begin{array}{lccc}
\text{country 1 starts at } y_1(0)/y^* & 0.8 & 1.0 & 2.0\\
\text{years until } y_1/y_2=1.1 & 21.4 & 27.4 & 49.9
\end{array}
\]
The log-linear figure of $37$ years is of the right order but not accurate: when both countries are below
the steady state the true convergence is faster (Exercise 3.3), and when both are above it is slower.

Either way the message is the one the book draws: the basic Solow model with a capital share of one third
predicts that a twofold income gap between otherwise identical countries is largely gone within a few
decades.  That is far faster than anything in the data --- the gaps documented in Chapter 1 have persisted
for a century and more --- which is one of the motivations for the augmented model of Section 3.3 (where a
larger effective capital share slows convergence) and, ultimately, for models in which $y^*$ itself differs
permanently across countries.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{3.5}{}
Index economies by $i$ and let each be a Solow economy with its own saving rate $s_i$, depreciation rate
$\delta_i$, population growth rate $n_i$ and technology level $A_i(t)$, with a common rate of technological
progress $g$ (so that a stable world income distribution is possible).  Each economy in isolation obeys
\bk{(3.7)}, so its steady-state effective capital-labor ratio $k_i^*$ solves
\[
s_i\frac{f(k_i^*)}{k_i^*}=\delta_i+g+n_i ,
\]
and its steady-state output per capita is $y_i^*(t)=A_i(t)f(k_i^*)$.

Repeating the derivation of \bk{(3.10)} economy by economy --- nothing in it used cross-country
homogeneity --- gives the country-specific convergence equation
\[
\frac{\dt y_i(t)}{y_i(t)}\approx g-\bigl(1-\varepsilon_k(k_i^*)\bigr)(\delta_i+g+n_i)
\bigl(\log y_i(t)-\log y_i^*(t)\bigr).
\]
Writing $\beta_i\equiv\bigl(1-\varepsilon_k(k_i^*)\bigr)(\delta_i+g+n_i)$ and discretizing between $t-1$
and $t$,
\[
g_{i,t,t-1}\approx\underbrace{\bigl[g+\beta_i\log y_i^*(t-1)\bigr]}_{\displaystyle b^0_i}
-\beta_i\log y_{i,t-1} .
\tag{$*$}
\]
This is \bk{(3.13)}, $g_{i,t,t-1}=b^0_i+b_1\log y_{i,t-1}+\varepsilon_{i,t}$, with $b_1=-\beta_i$: the
constant term is country specific, precisely because the steady state $y^*_i$ is.  Two observations complete
the derivation.

\emph{The slope is common when the elasticity is.}  With a Cobb--Douglas technology
$\varepsilon_k\equiv\alpha$ and $\beta_i=(1-\alpha)(\delta_i+g+n_i)$, so the slope varies across countries
only through $\delta_i+n_i$.  Since $\delta+n$ moves within a narrow range across countries while
$y^*_i$ varies enormously, treating $b_1$ as common and $b^0_i$ as country specific --- exactly what
\bk{(3.13)} does --- is the natural first approximation.  (That $b_1$ is in principle country specific is
the caveat the book records after \bk{(3.13)}.)

\emph{The intercept is a function of observable country characteristics.}  With Cobb--Douglas technology
$y^*_i(t)=A_i(t)\bigl[s_i/(\delta_i+g+n_i)\bigr]^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}$, so
\[
b^0_i=g+(1-\alpha)(\delta_i+g+n_i)\Bigl[\log A_i(t-1)
+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\bigl(\log s_i-\log(\delta_i+g+n_i)\bigr)\Bigr].
\]
Modeling $b^0_i$ as a linear function of $\log s_i$, $\log(\delta_i+g+n_i)$ and proxies for $\log A_i$ ---
schooling, institutions, openness, and the rest --- delivers the empirical specification \bk{(3.14)},
$g_{i,t,t-1}=X_{i,t}^{T}\beta+b_1\log y_{i,t-1}+\varepsilon_{i,t}$.  Conditional convergence is then the
statement that $b_1<0$ once these characteristics are held fixed, whereas unconditional convergence ---
the regression \bk{(3.12)} with a common intercept --- need not show $b_1<0$ at all: omitting $b^0_i$ puts
it in the error term, and since economies with low $y^*_i$ also tend to have low $y_{i,t-1}$, the resulting
$\mathrm{Cov}(b^0_i,\log y_{i,t-1})>0$ biases the estimate of $b_1$ upward.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{3.6}{}
The system to be studied is
\[
\dt k=G_k(k,h)\equiv s_kf(k,h)-(\delta_k+g+n)k,
\qquad
\dt h=G_h(k,h)\equiv s_hf(k,h)-(\delta_h+g+n)h ,
\]
on $\R_{++}^2$, where $f(k,h)=F(k,h,1)$ is strictly increasing and strictly concave in $(k,h)$ by
\bk{Assumption 1$'$} and satisfies the Inada conditions of \bk{Assumption 2$'$}.  By \bk{Proposition 3.1}
there is exactly one rest point $(k^*,h^*)\in\R_{++}^2$.  We must show that every solution starting in
$\R^2_{++}$ converges to it.

\emph{Step 1: the system is cooperative, hence the flow is monotone.}  The off-diagonal entries of the
Jacobian are
\[
\frac{\partial G_k}{\partial h}=s_kf_h(k,h)>0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial G_h}{\partial k}=s_hf_k(k,h)>0 ,
\]
so the vector field is \emph{cooperative}: each variable helps the other accumulate --- more human capital
raises output and therefore physical investment, and conversely.  By Kamke's comparison theorem the flow
preserves the componentwise order: if $(k_1(0),h_1(0))\le(k_2(0),h_2(0))$ then
$(k_1(t),h_1(t))\le(k_2(t),h_2(t))$ for all $t\ge0$.  Two consequences will be used repeatedly.  First, a
solution that starts above (below) $(k^*,h^*)$ stays above (below) it, since $(k^*,h^*)$ is itself a
solution.  Second, if the vector field is nonpositive in both coordinates at the initial point, then the
solution is nonincreasing in both coordinates for all $t$: the solution started at time $\tau>0$ lies below
the one started at time $0$, and both solve the same autonomous equation.

\emph{Step 2: a supersolution and a subsolution.}  Recall from the proof of \bk{Proposition 3.1} that the
loci $\{G_k=0\}$ and $\{G_h=0\}$ are increasing curves in $(k,h)$ space that cross exactly once, at
$(k^*,h^*)$, with $\{G_k=0\}$ steeper there; moreover $\{G_k=0\}$ lies below $\{G_h=0\}$ for small $(k,h)$
and above it for large $(k,h)$.  Since $G_k$ is increasing in $h$ and $G_h$ is increasing in $k$,
\[
G_k<0 \text{ to the right of } \{G_k=0\},
\qquad
G_h<0 \text{ above } \{G_h=0\} .
\]
For $k>k^*$ the region between the two curves is therefore a set on which $G_k<0$ \emph{and} $G_h<0$; it is
nonempty and, because both curves are increasing and unbounded (by \bk{Assumption 2$'$}, $f(k,h)/k\to0$ as
$k\to\infty$ forces $h\to\infty$ along $\{G_k=0\}$), it contains points $(\bar k,\bar h)$ with
$\bar k\ge k(0)$ and $\bar h\ge h(0)$, as large as we please.  Symmetrically, for $k<k^*$ the region between
the two curves has $G_k>0$ and $G_h>0$ and contains points $(\underline k,\underline h)$ with
$\underline k\le k(0)$, $\underline h\le h(0)$, as small as we please.

\emph{Step 3: the sandwich.}  Consider the solution started at $(\bar k,\bar h)$.  By Step 1 it is
nonincreasing in both coordinates, and it is bounded below by $(k^*,h^*)$ --- it started above the rest
point, so it stays above.  A monotone bounded trajectory converges, and its limit is a rest point of the
system; by uniqueness, the limit is $(k^*,h^*)$.  Exactly the same argument applied at
$(\underline k,\underline h)$ gives a trajectory that is nondecreasing, bounded above by $(k^*,h^*)$, and
convergent to it.  Finally, by monotonicity of the flow, the solution started at $(k(0),h(0))$ is trapped
between these two for all $t\ge0$:
\[
\bigl(\underline k(t),\underline h(t)\bigr)\le\bigl(k(t),h(t)\bigr)\le\bigl(\bar k(t),\bar h(t)\bigr),
\]
and both bounds converge to $(k^*,h^*)$.  Hence $(k(t),h(t))\to(k^*,h^*)$ from any initial condition in
$\R^2_{++}$, which is the assertion of \bk{Proposition 3.2}.

\rmk{The phase diagram in Figure 3.1 of the book is exactly this argument in pictures: the two loci cut the
positive quadrant into four regions, in two of which both variables move toward the steady state, while in
the other two the trajectory must cross into one of those.  What the monotone-flow argument adds is the
step the picture cannot supply --- that no trajectory can cycle around the rest point instead of converging
to it.  Cooperativity is what rules that out; for planar cooperative systems there are no closed orbits.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{3.7}{}
The steady state solves \bk{(3.17)}--\bk{(3.18)},
\[
\Phi_1(k,h)\equiv s_kf(k,h)-(\delta_k+g+n)k=0,
\qquad
\Phi_2(k,h)\equiv s_hf(k,h)-(\delta_h+g+n)h=0 .
\]
Write $J$ for the Jacobian of $(\Phi_1,\Phi_2)$ in $(k,h)$, evaluated at $(k^*,h^*)$:
\[
J=\begin{pmatrix}
s_kf_k-(\delta_k+g+n) & s_kf_h\\[2pt]
s_hf_k & s_hf_h-(\delta_h+g+n)
\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
J_{11}&J_{12}\\ J_{21}&J_{22}
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

\emph{Signs of the entries and of $\det J$.}  The off-diagonal entries are positive.  For the diagonal ones,
substitute $\delta_k+g+n=s_kf(k^*,h^*)/k^*$ from \bk{(3.17)}:
\[
J_{11}=s_k\Bigl[f_k(k^*,h^*)-\frac{f(k^*,h^*)}{k^*}\Bigr]<0 ,
\]
because strict concavity of $f$ in $k$ together with $f(0,h^*)\ge0$ gives $f>kf_k$; similarly $J_{22}<0$.
For the determinant, substituting both steady-state conditions,
\[
\det J=(\delta_k+g+n)(\delta_h+g+n)-s_kf_k(\delta_h+g+n)-s_hf_h(\delta_k+g+n)
=\frac{s_ks_hf}{k^*h^*}\bigl[f-k^*f_k-h^*f_h\bigr]>0 ,
\]
the bracket being positive by strict concavity of $f$ (this is the same inequality that made the two loci
cross only once in \bk{Proposition 3.1}).

\emph{The comparative statics.}  By the Implicit Function Theorem,
$\partial(k^*,h^*)^{T}/\partial\theta=-J^{-1}\,\partial(\Phi_1,\Phi_2)^{T}/\partial\theta$ with
\[
J^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det J}\begin{pmatrix}J_{22}&-J_{12}\\ -J_{21}&J_{11}\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Since $\partial\Phi/\partial s_k=(f,0)^{T}$, $\partial\Phi/\partial s_h=(0,f)^{T}$ and
$\partial\Phi/\partial n=(-k^*,-h^*)^{T}$,
\[
\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial s_k}=\frac{-J_{22}f}{\det J}>0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial h^*}{\partial s_k}=\frac{J_{21}f}{\det J}=\frac{s_hf_kf}{\det J}>0,
\]
\[
\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial s_h}=\frac{J_{12}f}{\det J}=\frac{s_kf_hf}{\det J}>0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial h^*}{\partial s_h}=\frac{-J_{11}f}{\det J}>0,
\]
\[
\frac{\partial k^*}{\partial n}=\frac{J_{22}k^*-J_{12}h^*}{\det J}<0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial h^*}{\partial n}=\frac{J_{11}h^*-J_{21}k^*}{\det J}<0 ,
\]
the last two because $J_{11},J_{22}<0$ while $J_{12},J_{21}>0$.  Since $f$ is increasing in both arguments,
output per effective unit of labor $\hat y^*=f(k^*,h^*)$ inherits the signs: it rises with either saving
rate and falls with population growth.

\emph{Interpretation.}  The novelty relative to the basic model is the \emph{cross} effect: a higher saving
rate in physical capital raises the steady-state stock of \emph{human} capital as well, and vice versa.  The
mechanism is the complementarity of the two factors in $f$ --- more physical capital raises output, and
since a constant fraction $s_h$ of output is invested in schooling, it raises human capital investment too.
This is the ``multiplier'' that makes the augmented model fit cross-country income differences so much
better than the basic one: the elasticity of steady-state income with respect to $s_k$ is
$\alpha/(1-\alpha-\beta)$ rather than $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$, as \bk{(3.22)}--\bk{(3.23)} show explicitly for
the Cobb--Douglas case.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{3.8}{}
\emph{The accounting equation.}  With $Y=F(K,H,AL)$, differentiate with respect to time and divide by $Y$:
\[
\frac{\dt Y}{Y}
=\frac{F_KK}{Y}\frac{\dt K}{K}
+\frac{F_HH}{Y}\frac{\dt H}{H}
+\frac{F_{AL}\,AL}{Y}\Bigl(\frac{\dt A}{A}+\frac{\dt L}{L}\Bigr),
\]
where $F_{AL}$ is the derivative with respect to the third argument.  With competitive factor markets the
elasticities are the observed income shares: $R=F_K$ gives $\alpha_K=F_KK/Y$; the rental rate of human
capital $R_H=F_H$ gives $\alpha_H=F_HH/Y$; and the wage per worker is $w=AF_{AL}$, so
$\alpha_L=wL/Y=F_{AL}AL/Y$.  Constant returns in the three arguments make the three shares sum to one.
Writing $g_X$ for the growth rate of $X$,
\[
\boxed{\ g_Y=\alpha_Kg_K+\alpha_Hg_H+\alpha_L\bigl(g_A+g_L\bigr)\ }
\]
so that the estimate of TFP growth --- the part of output growth not accounted for by measured inputs --- is
\[
\hat x=g_Y-\alpha_Kg_K-\alpha_Hg_H-\alpha_Lg_L ,
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
g_A=\frac{\hat x}{\alpha_L} .
\]
Two differences from the fundamental growth accounting equation \bk{(3.3)} of the basic model deserve
emphasis.  First, the accumulation of human capital is now an \emph{input}, so growth in schooling and
skills is no longer part of the residual.  Second, because technology is labor augmenting, the residual
$\hat x$ measures $\alpha_L g_A$, not $g_A$: with a labor share of about $2/3$, the implied rate of
technological progress is one and a half times the measured residual.

\emph{Using data.}  One needs (i) output growth $g_Y$, from national accounts; (ii) capital stock growth
$g_K$, from a perpetual inventory calculation on investment series; (iii) growth in efficiency units of
labor $g_H$, constructed as in Section 3.5 from schooling data and Mincerian returns, and $g_L$ from
employment; and (iv) the three factor shares.  The last item is the binding constraint, and it is where the
exercise bites: national accounts report a split of value added into capital income and \emph{labor}
income, but labor income here is the sum of two conceptually different payments, $R_HH$ and $wL$.  Some
identifying assumption is unavoidable.  The two used in practice are
\begin{itemize}
\item impose a functional form and calibrate: with $Y=K^{\alpha}H^{\beta}(AL)^{1-\alpha-\beta}$ as in
      \bk{(3.21)}, the shares are the constants $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $1-\alpha-\beta$, and $\beta$ is
      pinned down from microeconometric (Mincerian) estimates of the return to schooling; or
\item avoid the split altogether by folding human capital into effective labor, $Y=F(K,AH)$, as in
      \bk{(3.29)}: then only two shares are needed, $\alpha_K$ and $1-\alpha_K$, and the accounting
      equation collapses to $g_Y=\alpha_Kg_K+(1-\alpha_K)(g_A+g_H)$, at the price of imposing that raw
      labor and human capital are perfect substitutes in efficiency units.
\end{itemize}
Either way the residual absorbs everything the measurement misses --- quality of schooling, capacity
utilization, reallocation across sectors and firms, and pure measurement error --- so it remains, in
Abramovitz's phrase, a measure of our ignorance; adding human capital narrows that ignorance rather than
eliminating it.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{3.11}{}
\emph{(a) The accounting.}  Work in per worker terms, $y_j\equiv Y_j/L_j$ and $\kappa_j\equiv K_j/L_j$.
Constant returns make output per worker a function of the capital-labor ratio and technology alone,
$y=\phi(\kappa;A)$, and competitive markets make the elasticity of that function equal to the \emph{observed}
capital share:
\[
\frac{\partial\log y}{\partial\log\kappa}=\frac{F_KK}{Y}=\alpha^{K} .
\]
This is all that is needed: no functional form, no assumption about how $A$ enters.  Order the countries by
their capital-labor ratio, $\kappa_{(1)}\le\kappa_{(2)}\le\dots\le\kappa_{(J)}$.  If two adjacent countries
had the same technology, the whole of their income difference would have to come from capital deepening,
\[
\Delta\log y_{\text{capital}}=\bar\alpha^{K}\,\Delta\log\kappa,
\qquad
\bar\alpha^{K}=\tfrac12\bigl(\alpha^{K}_{(i)}+\alpha^{K}_{(i+1)}\bigr),
\]
a Divisia (T\"ornqvist) index that uses the average of the two observed shares over the interval.  The
residual is the estimated technology gap,
\[
\log\frac{A_{(i+1)}}{A_{(i)}}
=\bigl(\log y_{(i+1)}-\log y_{(i)}\bigr)-\bar\alpha^{K}\bigl(\log\kappa_{(i+1)}-\log\kappa_{(i)}\bigr),
\]
and cumulating these differences along the ordered cross section gives every country's productivity
relative to a numeraire country (the United States, say).  Equivalently, and in one step for any pair,
\[
\log\frac{A_j}{A_{\text{US}}}
=\log\frac{y_j}{y_{\text{US}}}-\bar\alpha^{K}\log\frac{\kappa_j}{\kappa_{\text{US}}} .
\]
The contribution of technology to cross-country income differences is then measured by the dispersion of
$\log A_j$ relative to the dispersion of $\log y_j$, and the ``predicted'' income of country $j$ under a
common technology is $\log\hat y_j=\log y_{\text{US}}+\bar\alpha^{K}\log(\kappa_j/\kappa_{\text{US}})$.
(With a common Cobb--Douglas technology $\alpha^K_j\equiv\alpha$ and this is exactly the calibration of
Section 3.5.)  Only if the shares vary across countries does the chaining matter, and then the index is
exact only up to the usual path dependence, which is second order when the share varies slowly.

\emph{(b) What do large $A$ differences mean?}  Not necessarily that countries have access to different
production possibility sets.  The residual $A_j$ is defined as whatever makes the accounting identity hold,
so it absorbs every reason why a country produces less from the \emph{measured} inputs than the numeraire
country does:
\begin{itemize}
\item unmeasured or mismeasured inputs --- human capital (part (c)), land and natural resources,
      capital quality and capacity utilization, hours per worker;
\item allocative efficiency --- misallocation of capital and labor across firms and sectors, distortions,
      credit constraints, monopoly power, informality;
\item organization and institutions --- the efficiency with which given inputs are combined;
\item genuinely different technical knowledge or its adoption.
\end{itemize}
Only the last is technology in the engineering sense.  Since a country can, in principle, adopt known
technologies, the finding of very large $A$ gaps is better read as a statement that \emph{something other
than the accumulation of measured factors} accounts for most of cross-country income differences --- which
is precisely what motivates Chapter 4's search for fundamental causes.

\emph{(c) What data are needed with $F(K,H,A)$?}  Efficiency units of labor must be measured: the schooling
distribution of the workforce (attainment by level), an estimate of how schooling maps into productivity
(Mincerian returns, ideally allowing the return to vary by level and country), experience profiles, and
--- if one wants $\alpha_H$ separately from $\alpha_L$ --- the split of labor income into payments to raw
labor and to skills.  Data on schooling quality (test scores, expenditure per pupil, teacher quality) are
needed to avoid the bias in part (d), as are hours worked if labor input is to be measured consistently.

\emph{(d) Quality of schooling shows up as productivity.}  Suppose the truth is
$Y_j=K_j^{\alpha}\bigl(A H_j\bigr)^{1-\alpha}$ with a \emph{common} $A$, and that the true stock of
efficiency units is $H_j=Q_j\wh H_j$, where $\wh H_j$ is the measure constructed from years of schooling as
in Section 3.5 and $Q_j>0$ captures the quality of a year of schooling.  The calibration of Section 3.5
computes productivity as the residual from the measured stock $\wh H_j$:
\[
\wh A_j\equiv\Bigl[\frac{Y_j}{K_j^{\alpha}\wh H_j^{\,1-\alpha}}\Bigr]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
=\Bigl[\frac{K_j^{\alpha}(AQ_j\wh H_j)^{1-\alpha}}{K_j^{\alpha}\wh H_j^{\,1-\alpha}}
\Bigr]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
=A\,Q_j .
\]
So the estimated productivity of country $j$ relative to the United States is
\[
\frac{\wh A_j}{\wh A_{\text{US}}}=\frac{Q_j}{Q_{\text{US}}} ,
\]
even though all countries share the same technology: \emph{every} estimated productivity difference is
schooling quality in disguise.  The bias goes the empirically awkward way, too.  Poor countries are
generally thought to have lower $Q$, so $\wh A_j$ is understated for them, and the calibration attributes
to ``technology'' what is really an unmeasured input --- which is exactly the criticism of the Hall--Jones
and Klenow--Rodr\'iguez calibrations that the quality-adjustment literature (Caselli, 2005) takes up.
\end{aex}

\solchapter{4}{Fundamental Determinants of Differences in Economic Performance}

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.86\textwidth}
\small
The two exercises of Chapter 4 left unsolved in the instructor's manual: Exercises 4.1 and 4.2, both on the
Simon--Kremer model of Section 4.2.  The manual solves 4.3.  Throughout, world output is
$Y(t)=L(t)^{\alpha}\bigl(A(t)Z\bigr)^{1-\alpha}$ with $Z$ normalized to $1$, so that
$Y=L^{\alpha}A^{1-\alpha}$, and population responds to output through \bk{(4.2)}, $L(t)=\phi Y(t)$.
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\bigskip
\hrule
\bigskip

\begin{aex}{4.1}{}
\emph{Population as a function of the stock of ideas.}  Substituting the production function into
\bk{(4.2)},
\[
L=\phi Y=\phi L^{\alpha}A^{1-\alpha}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
L^{1-\alpha}=\phi A^{1-\alpha}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
L(t)=\phi^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}A(t) .
\tag{$*$}
\]
The Malthusian channel thus ties population to the stock of knowledge one for one: any improvement in
technology is absorbed by a proportional increase in the number of people it has to feed.

\emph{Equation \bk{(4.3)}.}  Each individual discovers ideas at the rate $\lambda$, so by \bk{(4.1)}
$\dt A=\lambda L$.  Substituting ($*$),
\[
\dt A(t)=\lambda\phi^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}A(t),
\]
which is \bk{(4.3)}: the \emph{level} of the flow of new ideas is proportional to the existing stock, because
the population that generates them is.

\emph{Equation \bk{(4.4)}.}  This is a linear differential equation with constant coefficient; separating
variables, $\dd\log A=\lambda\phi^{1/(1-\alpha)}\dd t$, so with the initial condition $A(0)>0$,
\[
A(t)=\exp\bigl(\lambda\phi^{1/(1-\alpha)}t\bigr)A(0),
\]
which is \bk{(4.4)}.  Technology grows exponentially at the constant rate $g\equiv\lambda\phi^{1/(1-\alpha)}$,
and so do population, by ($*$), and world output, since
\[
Y=L^{\alpha}A^{1-\alpha}=\bigl(\phi^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}A\bigr)^{\alpha}A^{1-\alpha}
=\phi^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}A .
\]

\rmk{Income per capita, however, does not grow at all: $y=Y/L=1/\phi$ at every date, directly from
\bk{(4.2)}.  The model produces a growing \emph{world} --- more people, more ideas, more output --- with
living standards pinned down by the Malthusian relation between income and population.  That is the sense
in which economies of scale in population are a theory of world growth rather than of prosperity, and it is
the observation that Exercise 4.3(a) formalizes.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{4.2}{}
\emph{Derivation.}  Simon and Kremer replace \bk{(4.1)} by the assumption that population determines the
\emph{proportional} rate of improvement,
\[
\frac{\dt A(t)}{A(t)}=\lambda L(t).
\]
The static relations are unchanged, so ($*$) of Exercise 4.1 still gives
$L(t)=\phi^{1/(1-\alpha)}A(t)$ and therefore
\[
\dt A(t)=\lambda\phi^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}A(t)^{2}.
\]
This is separable: $\dd A/A^{2}=\lambda\phi^{1/(1-\alpha)}\dd t$, and integrating from $0$ to $t$,
\[
\frac{1}{A(0)}-\frac{1}{A(t)}=\lambda\phi^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}t
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
A(t)=\frac{1}{A(0)^{-1}-\lambda\phi^{1/(1-\alpha)}t} ,
\]
which is \bk{(4.5)}.

\emph{How the behavior differs.}  Under \bk{(4.4)} the growth rate of technology is the constant
$\lambda\phi^{1/(1-\alpha)}$.  Under \bk{(4.5)} it is
\[
\frac{\dt A(t)}{A(t)}=\lambda\phi^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}A(t)
=\frac{\lambda\phi^{1/(1-\alpha)}}{A(0)^{-1}-\lambda\phi^{1/(1-\alpha)}t},
\]
which is increasing in $t$: growth accelerates.  Indeed the acceleration is violent.  The denominator of
\bk{(4.5)} vanishes at
\[
T^{*}=\frac{1}{\lambda\phi^{1/(1-\alpha)}A(0)}<\infty ,
\]
so technology, population and output all become infinite in \emph{finite} time; the solution does not
merely grow fast, it blows up.  Starting from a small $A(0)$, $T^{*}$ is large and the early path is nearly
flat, which is what gives the model its appeal as a story of millennia of stagnation followed by a takeoff.

\emph{Why.}  The difference is in how an idea is assumed to interact with the stock of ideas already
available.  Under \bk{(4.1)} a discovery adds a fixed increment to the pool: a person is worth $\lambda$
units of $A$ per unit of time regardless of how much is already known.  Under the Simon--Kremer assumption a
discovery raises the stock \emph{proportionally}: each person contributes $\lambda$ percentage points of
growth per unit of time, so a discovery is worth more the more is already known --- the ``standing on the
shoulders of giants'' externality.  Since the Malthusian channel makes population itself proportional to
$A$, the second assumption makes the growth rate proportional to $A$, and a differential equation whose
growth rate grows linearly in the state explodes in finite time.

\emph{Which is more plausible?}  Neither is comfortable, and they fail in different places.

Taken as a description of the very long run, the Simon--Kremer version is the better one.  It is
essentially the only simple mechanism that delivers the qualitative shape of the historical record ---
hundreds of millennia of near-stagnation followed by an acceleration --- and Kremer's evidence that the
growth rate of world population has risen with the \emph{level} of world population over a million years is
direct support for the proportional specification.

Taken literally as a model, however, it cannot be right.  Finite-time blow-up is not a prediction about any
observable world, so the specification must fail at some scale; and the mechanism is a pure scale effect,
so it predicts that larger populations grow faster --- a prediction rejected when the units are countries
(the European population was below that of Asia for two millennia, yet modern growth began in Europe), and
rescued only by insisting that the relevant unit is the world as a whole.

The assumption in \bk{(4.1)} has the more defensible microfoundation --- independent discoveries arriving
at a constant rate per person, which is exactly the probabilistic story the section tells --- but it can
only generate steady exponential growth, never a takeoff.

The binding weakness, though, is shared and lies elsewhere: it is \bk{(4.2)}.  Both models force income per
capita to equal $1/\phi$ at every date, so neither can speak to living standards at all, and the
Malthusian link between income and population on which they rest broke down precisely when modern growth
began, with the demographic transition reversing the sign of the relation between income and fertility.  A
mechanism that is silent about income per capita and about \emph{where} growth started is, as the chapter
concludes, at best a theory of world growth --- not a fundamental cause of cross-country differences.
\end{aex}

\solchapter{5}{Foundations of Neoclassical Growth}

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.86\textwidth}
\small
The six exercises of Chapter 5 left unsolved in the instructor's manual: Exercises 5.3--5.8.  The manual
solves 5.1, 5.2 and 5.9--5.14.  Notation follows the book: $\mathcal H$ is the set of households,
$p=(p_1,\dots,p_N)$ the price vector, $w^h$ household $h$'s income, $v^h(p,w^h)$ its indirect utility,
$\omega^h$ its endowment and $\theta^h_f$ its share in firm $f$.
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\bigskip
\hrule
\bigskip

\begin{aex}{5.3}{}
Each household $h\in\mathcal H$ has indirect utility of the Gorman form \bk{(5.3)},
\[
v^h(p,w^h)=a^h(p)+b(p)w^h ,
\]
with a common $b(\cdot)$, and a positive demand for every commodity.  We must show that aggregate demand is
the demand of a single household with indirect utility $v(p,w)=a(p)+b(p)w$, where
$a(p)\equiv\int_{h\in\mathcal H}a^h(p)\dd h$ and $w\equiv\int_{h\in\mathcal H}w^h\dd h$.

\emph{Step 1: what homogeneity forces.}  Any indirect utility function is homogeneous of degree $0$ in
$(p,w)$, so for every $\lambda>0$ and every $w^h$,
\[
a^h(\lambda p)+b(\lambda p)\lambda w^h=a^h(p)+b(p)w^h .
\]
Both sides are affine in $w^h$, and the household's income can be varied freely, so the constant terms and
the slopes must agree separately:
\[
a^h(\lambda p)=a^h(p)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
b(\lambda p)=\lambda^{-1}b(p) ,
\]
that is, $a^h$ is homogeneous of degree $0$ and $b$ of degree $-1$.  Since $v^h$ is strictly increasing in
income, $b(p)>0$.

\emph{Step 2: individual demands.}  Assume $a^h$ and $b$ differentiable.  By Roy's Identity, household
$h$'s demand for commodity $j$ is
\[
x^h_j(p,w^h)
=-\frac{\partial v^h/\partial p_j}{\partial v^h/\partial w^h}
=-\frac{1}{b(p)}\frac{\partial a^h(p)}{\partial p_j}
-\frac{1}{b(p)}\frac{\partial b(p)}{\partial p_j}w^h .
\tag{$*$}
\]
(The hypothesis that each household consumes a positive amount of every commodity is what makes the
demands interior, so that Roy's Identity applies and no household sits at a corner where the envelope
argument would fail.)  Note the structure: the Engel curve of household $h$ for commodity $j$ is a straight
line in $w^h$ whose \emph{slope}, $-b^{-1}\partial b/\partial p_j$, does not depend on $h$.  All the
household-specific information sits in the intercept.

\emph{Step 3: aggregation.}  Integrating ($*$) over $\mathcal H$ and exchanging differentiation and
integration,
\begin{align*}
x_j(p)\equiv\int_{h\in\mathcal H}x^h_j(p,w^h)\dd h
&=-\frac{1}{b(p)}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_j}\int_{h\in\mathcal H}a^h(p)\dd h
-\frac{1}{b(p)}\frac{\partial b(p)}{\partial p_j}\int_{h\in\mathcal H}w^h\dd h\\
&=-\frac{1}{b(p)}\frac{\partial a(p)}{\partial p_j}-\frac{1}{b(p)}\frac{\partial b(p)}{\partial p_j}w .
\end{align*}
Now consider the hypothetical household with indirect utility $v(p,w)=a(p)+b(p)w$.  It is a legitimate
indirect utility function: $a$ is homogeneous of degree $0$ and $b$ of degree $-1$ by Step 1, so $v$ is
homogeneous of degree $0$; it is increasing in $w$ and decreasing in each $p_j$ wherever the individual
ones are.  Applying Roy's Identity to it gives demands
\[
-\frac{1}{b(p)}\frac{\partial a(p)}{\partial p_j}-\frac{1}{b(p)}\frac{\partial b(p)}{\partial p_j}w ,
\]
which is exactly $x_j(p)$.  Hence aggregate demand is generated by the maximization problem of the single
household with indirect utility $v(p,w)$: the economy admits a representative household, and
\bk{Theorem 5.2} is proved.

\emph{Step 4: the representative household is \emph{strong}.}  Aggregate demand depends on the distribution
of income only through the aggregate $w=\int w^h\dd h$, so any redistribution of income across households
leaves it unchanged.  The converse also holds, which is why the Gorman form is not merely sufficient but
necessary: if two households $h$ and $h'$ had Engel curves of different slopes for commodity $j$, that is
$b_h^{-1}\partial b_h/\partial p_j\neq b_{h'}^{-1}\partial b_{h'}/\partial p_j$, then transferring a unit of
income from $h'$ to $h$ would change aggregate demand for $j$, and no single household's demand function
could reproduce aggregate demand at all income distributions.

\rmk{Nothing in the argument used $N<\infty$ beyond writing the derivatives coordinate by coordinate, and
nothing used $\mathcal H$ finite: the integral is a Lebesgue integral and covers a continuum of households.
What the theorem does \emph{not} deliver is a \emph{normative} representative household --- the utility
$v(p,w)$ reproduces aggregate demand but need not rank allocations the way any Pareto criterion would.
That requires the additional structure of \bk{Theorem 5.3}, in particular $a^h(p)=a^h$ independent of $p$
for the converse direction.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{5.4}{}
Let the commodity space be indexed by $j\in J\equiv[0,1]$ with the Lebesgue measure (nothing depends on the
particular index set; $J=\R_+$ for a continuous-time horizon reads the same way).  A consumption bundle is
a measurable function $x:J\to\R$, and we take the commodity space to be $L^{\infty}(J)$ with prices in its
predual $L^{1}(J)$, so that the value of a bundle is
\[
p\cdot x=\int_{J}p(j)x(j)\dd j ,
\]
which is finite for $p\in L^{1}$, $x\in L^{\infty}$.  (Any dual pair with a bilinear form of this kind will
do; what matters is that values are given by integrals and that the dual space is rich enough to separate
points.)  Household $h$'s preferences have the Gorman representation
$v^h(p,w^h)=a^h(p)+b(p)w^h$, each household demands a positive amount of (almost) every commodity, and the
aggregate production possibilities set $Y\subset L^{\infty}(J)$ is convex.  The claim is that
\bk{Theorem 5.3} survives verbatim:
\begin{enumerate}
\item any feasible allocation that maximizes $v(p,w)=\int_{\mathcal H}a^h(p)\dd h+b(p)w$, with
      $w=\int_{\mathcal H}w^h\dd h$, is Pareto optimal;
\item if in addition $a^h(p)=a^h$ for all $p$ and all $h$, then every Pareto optimal allocation maximizes
      $v(p,w)$.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{What carries over unchanged.}  The proof of \bk{Theorem 5.3} is an argument about the \emph{objective}
\[
\max\ \sum_{h\in\mathcal H}\alpha^hv^h(p,w^h)
=\max\ \Bigl[\int_{\mathcal H}\alpha^ha^h(p)\dd h+b(p)\int_{\mathcal H}\alpha^hw^h\dd h\Bigr]
\]
subject to market clearing and to net supplies being profit maximizing at $p$, exactly as in \bk{(5.7)}.
Every step of it is linear in the commodity index: sums $\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_jy_j$ become integrals
$\int_Jp(j)y(j)\dd j$, and Roy's Identity becomes the same formula with the functional (G\^ateaux)
derivatives $\delta a^h(p)/\delta p(j)$ and $\delta b(p)/\delta p(j)$ in place of partial derivatives.  The
linearity of the Gorman form in $w^h$ --- which is what makes the Pareto weights enter only through the
weighted aggregate income and lets one substitute income transfers for commodity reallocations --- is a
statement about incomes, not about commodities, so it is untouched.  With $b(p)>0$, the objective is still
increasing in aggregate income, and part 1 follows as before; with $a^h(p)=a^h$ constant, the argument by
contradiction that produced \bk{(5.9)}--\bk{(5.11)} goes through with integrals.

\emph{What genuinely needs an extra assumption.}  Two things that are automatic in $\R^N$ are not automatic
here.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Existence of supporting prices.}  In $\R^N$ a convex set can always be separated from a
      disjoint convex set.  In an infinite-dimensional space the separating hyperplane theorem requires a
      topological condition --- for instance that $Y$ (or the set of preferred allocations) have nonempty
      interior in the sup-norm topology, or that preferences satisfy Mas-Colell's properness condition.
      Without it a Pareto optimum need not be supported by any price system in the chosen dual space, and
      the phrase ``at price vector $p$'' in the theorem would be empty.  This is the same hypothesis the
      book adds in \bk{Theorems 5.6} and \bk{5.7} when it proves the Second Welfare Theorem with infinitely
      many commodities; here it is needed for exactly the same reason.
\item \emph{Integrability.}  The aggregates $a(p)=\int_{\mathcal H}a^h(p)\dd h$ and
      $w=\int_{\mathcal H}w^h\dd h$ must be finite, and the interchange of $\delta/\delta p(j)$ with
      $\int_{\mathcal H}\dd h$ must be legitimate --- guaranteed, for instance, by a uniform integrable
      bound on the family $\{\delta a^h(p)/\delta p(j)\}$.  With a continuum of commodities one must also
      require $p\cdot x^h<\infty$ for each household, which Exercise 5.5 delivers from local
      nonsatiation.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Conclusion.}  With these two provisos --- a price system exists in the dual, and the household
aggregates are well defined --- \bk{Theorem 5.3} holds word for word with $\sum_{j=1}^N$ replaced by
$\int_J\dd j$.  The economics of the result is therefore robust to the infinite-dimensional setting that
growth models live in: it is the \emph{linearity of Gorman preferences in income}, not the finiteness of
the commodity space, that makes the representative household normative.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{5.5}{}
``Household $h$ chooses $x^h$ at prices $p$'' means that $x^h$ solves
\[
\max_{x\in X^h}\ U^h(x)
\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
p\cdot x\le w^h,
\qquad
w^h\equiv p\cdot\Bigl(\omega^h+\sum_{f\in\mathcal F}\theta^h_fy^f\Bigr),
\]
and local nonsatiation is \bk{Definition 5.3}: at every $x\in X^h$, $U^h$ is strictly increasing in at least
one of its arguments, and $U^h(x)<\infty$.

\emph{Step 1: the income of a locally nonsatiated household that makes a choice is finite.}  Suppose
$w^h=\infty$.  Then the budget constraint does not bind anywhere and the problem reduces to maximizing
$U^h$ over $X^h$.  But local nonsatiation says that at \emph{every} point of $X^h$ there is a coordinate
$j'$ in which $U^h$ is strictly increasing, so for any candidate $x$ the bundle $x(\varepsilon)$ that adds
$\varepsilon>0$ to coordinate $j'$ --- which is in $X^h$ for small $\varepsilon$, this being exactly the
construction used in the proof of \bk{Theorem 5.5} --- satisfies $U^h(x(\varepsilon))>U^h(x)$ and is still
affordable.  No maximum exists, so no bundle is chosen, contradicting the hypothesis.  Hence $w^h<\infty$.

\emph{Step 2: the chosen bundle is affordable, hence of finite value.}  Since $x^h$ is in the budget set,
\[
p\cdot x^h\le w^h<\infty .
\]

\emph{Step 3: in fact the budget holds with equality.}  Suppose $p\cdot x^h<w^h$.  By local nonsatiation
there is a coordinate $j'$ in which $U^h$ is strictly increasing at $x^h$; for $\varepsilon$ small enough
the bundle $x^h(\varepsilon)$ obtained by adding $\varepsilon$ to that coordinate still costs less than
$w^h$ and yields strictly higher utility, contradicting optimality.  Therefore
\[
p\cdot x^h=w^h<\infty .
\]

\rmk{In a finite-dimensional economy this is a triviality --- a finite sum of finite terms.  Its content is
in the infinite-dimensional case relevant to growth theory, where $p\cdot x=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}p_tx_t$ is
an infinite series that may diverge.  The result says that at any chosen bundle the series converges, and
this is precisely what the proof of the First Welfare Theorem needs: \bk{(5.17)}--\bk{(5.18)} are summed
over households, and the sums are meaningless unless each term is finite.  It is also the reason
\bk{Definition 5.3} builds $U^h(x^h)<\infty$ into local nonsatiation: an infinite-utility plan would allow a
household to be indifferent to budget constraints, and both the definition of a competitive equilibrium and
the welfare theorems would lose their force.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{5.6}{}
\emph{(a) Demands and indirect utility.}  Write $\hat x^h_j\equiv x^h_j-\xi^h_j$ and
$\hat w^h\equiv w^h-\sum_{j}p_j\xi^h_j>0$ (positive by the assumption
$\sum_jp_j\bar\xi<w^h$).  The problem
\[
\max\ \Bigl[\sum_{j=1}^N(\hat x^h_j)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}\Bigr]^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}}
\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad
\sum_{j=1}^Np_j\hat x^h_j\le\hat w^h
\]
is the textbook CES problem.  The first-order conditions give
$(\hat x^h_j)^{-1/\sigma}=\mu p_j$ for a multiplier $\mu>0$, hence
$\hat x^h_j\propto p_j^{-\sigma}$, and the budget constraint fixes the factor:
\[
\hat x^h_j=\frac{\hat w^h\,p_j^{-\sigma}}{P},
\qquad
P\equiv\sum_{k=1}^Np_k^{1-\sigma},
\qquad\text{that is}\qquad
x^h_j=\xi^h_j+\frac{\bigl(w^h-\sum_kp_k\xi^h_k\bigr)p_j^{-\sigma}}{\sum_kp_k^{1-\sigma}} .
\]
Substituting back,
\[
\sum_j(\hat x^h_j)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}
=\Bigl(\frac{\hat w^h}{P}\Bigr)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}\sum_jp_j^{1-\sigma}
=\Bigl(\frac{\hat w^h}{P}\Bigr)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}P ,
\]
so that
\[
v^h(p,w^h)=\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{\hat w^h}{P}\Bigr)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}P\Bigr]^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}}
=\hat w^h\,P^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}
=\frac{-\sum_{j}p_j\xi^h_j+w^h}{\bigl[\sum_{j}p_j^{1-\sigma}\bigr]^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}} ,
\]
which is \bk{(5.5)}.  It is of the Gorman form \bk{(5.3)} with
\[
b(p)=P^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}>0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
a^h(p)=-\Bigl(\sum_jp_j\xi^h_j\Bigr)P^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}} ,
\]
and the household-specific term enters only through $a^h$, exactly as \bk{Theorem 5.2} requires.  It is
homogeneous of degree $0$: $\hat w^h$ is homogeneous of degree $1$ in $(p,w)$ and $P^{1/(\sigma-1)}$ of
degree $-1$.

\emph{(b) The representative household.}  The utility function \bk{(5.6)} is the same CES form with
$\xi_j\equiv\int_{\mathcal H}\xi^h_j\dd h$, so the computation in (a) applies verbatim and gives
\[
v(p,w)=\frac{-\sum_jp_j\xi_j+w}{\bigl[\sum_jp_j^{1-\sigma}\bigr]^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}}}
=\int_{\mathcal H}a^h(p)\dd h+b(p)w ,
\]
using $\int_{\mathcal H}a^h(p)\dd h=-\bigl(\sum_jp_j\xi_j\bigr)P^{1/(\sigma-1)}$ and
$w=\int_{\mathcal H}w^h\dd h$.  This is precisely the indirect utility of the representative household
constructed in \bk{Theorem 5.2}, so \bk{(5.6)} is the utility function that rationalizes aggregate demand.

\emph{(c) Dropping the outer exponent.}  Let now
$\wt U^h=\sum_{j}(x^h_j-\xi^h_j)^{(\sigma-1)/\sigma}$.  Take $\sigma>1$, so that
$(\sigma-1)/\sigma>0$ and $\wt U^h=(U^h)^{(\sigma-1)/\sigma}$ is a strictly increasing transformation of the
utility in part (a).  Demands are therefore unchanged, and the indirect utility function is the same
transformation of \bk{(5.5)}:
\[
\wt v^h(p,w^h)=\bigl[\hat w^hP^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}\bigr]^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}
=\bigl(w^h-\textstyle\sum_jp_j\xi^h_j\bigr)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}P^{\frac{1}{\sigma}} .
\]
It is still homogeneous of degree $0$ --- the first factor has degree $(\sigma-1)/\sigma$ and the second
$(1-\sigma)/\sigma$ --- but it is \emph{not} of the Gorman form: it is a strictly concave, not affine,
function of $w^h$, so the Engel curves are no longer straight lines.  The monotone transformation
$z\mapsto z^{\sigma/(\sigma-1)}$ restores \bk{(5.5)} and with it the Gorman form.

\emph{Is that enough?}  For the positive question --- does the economy admit a (strong) representative
household? --- yes.  Demand is an ordinal object: it is invariant to strictly increasing transformations of
utility, so what matters is whether \emph{some} representation of preferences takes the Gorman form, not
whether the one we happened to write down does.  This is the point the book makes right after
\bk{Theorem 5.2}, and part (c) is the example that makes it concrete: aggregate demand here is exactly as in
part (b).

Two caveats keep the answer from being an unqualified yes.  First, under uncertainty utility is cardinal:
the transformation $z\mapsto z^{\sigma/(\sigma-1)}$ changes the household's attitude to risk, so two
representations that are equivalent for a deterministic exchange economy are \emph{not} equivalent once
expected utility is taken over lotteries --- the aggregation result survives only for the representation
that is also the Bernoulli utility index.  Second, for a \emph{normative} representative household the
transformation matters as well: \bk{Theorem 5.3}(2) requires the Gorman representation to have
$a^h(p)=a^h$ independent of prices, which is a restriction on a particular cardinalization, and Pareto
weights attached to $\wt v^h$ and to $v^h$ define different social objectives.  Aggregation of demand is
ordinal; welfare analysis is not.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{5.7}{}
\emph{Survival.}  Death arrives as the first event of a Poisson process with intensity $\nu$, so the
random date of death $T$ is exponentially distributed:
\[
\Pr\{T>t\}=e^{-\nu t},
\qquad
\E[T]=\frac1\nu<\infty .
\]
The individual's expected lifetime is $1/\nu$, which is how one calibrates $\nu$.

\emph{Expected utility.}  Normalize the flow utility after death to $u(0)=0$, and let $[c(t)]_{t\ge0}$ be
the consumption plan conditional on being alive.  Discounting the future at the pure rate $\rho$, expected
lifetime utility at date $0$ is
\[
U_0=\E\Bigl[\int_0^{T}e^{-\rho t}u(c(t))\dd t\Bigr]
=\E\Bigl[\int_0^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}u(c(t))\,\mathbf{1}\{T>t\}\dd t\Bigr] .
\]
The integrand is nonnegative and measurable, so Fubini's theorem allows the expectation to be taken inside:
\[
U_0=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}u(c(t))\Pr\{T>t\}\dd t
=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}e^{-\nu t}u(c(t))\dd t
=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-(\rho+\nu)t}u(c(t))\dd t .
\]
This is the objective of an infinitely lived individual with discount rate
\[
\boxed{\ \rho_{\text{eff}}=\rho+\nu\ }
\]
Since the constraint set --- the flow budget constraint and the no-Ponzi condition --- is unaffected by the
\emph{form} of the objective, the individual's problem is identical to that of an infinitely lived agent
who discounts at $\rho+\nu$.  Her behavior, in particular her Euler equation
$\dt c/c=\bigl(r(t)-\rho-\nu\bigr)/\theta$ for the CRRA case, is that of the infinite-horizon model with the
larger discount rate.

\emph{Consistency with \bk{(5.12)}.}  In discrete time the book obtains
$\beta=\hat\beta(1-\nu)$; writing $\hat\beta=e^{-\rho\Delta}$ and $1-\nu\approx e^{-\nu\Delta}$ for a
period of length $\Delta$ gives $\beta\approx e^{-(\rho+\nu)\Delta}$, which is the same statement: the
probability of not surviving acts exactly like impatience.

\rmk{The result says that mortality raises the \emph{effective discount rate}, not that it makes people
poorer.  Whether it also changes consumption growth depends on the asset market.  With actuarially fair
annuities --- the Blanchard--Yaari setting of Chapter 9 --- a surviving individual earns $r+\nu$ rather than
$r$ on her savings, because the assets of those who die are shared among the survivors, and the two effects
cancel in the individual Euler equation: $\dt c/c=\bigl[(r+\nu)-(\rho+\nu)\bigr]/\theta=(r-\rho)/\theta$.
What mortality then changes is the \emph{aggregate}, through the arrival of newborns who hold no assets,
which is exactly why the perpetual youth model breaks Ricardian equivalence while the individual problem
looks infinite horizon.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{5.8}{}
\emph{(a) Different utility functions across generations.}  Yes --- the dynastic recursion still collapses
into an infinite-horizon maximization, but the problem loses stationarity.  Let generation $t$ value its
own consumption through $u_t$ and the continuation value of its descendant with factor $\beta$:
\[
V_t(y_t)=\max_{c_t+b_t\le y_t}\bigl\{u_t(c_t)+\beta V_{t+1}(b_t+w)\bigr\} .
\]
Iterating forward,
\[
V_0=\max\ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^tu_t(c_t),
\]
provided the sum converges (e.g.\ if the $u_t$ are uniformly bounded).  So a dynasty of one-period-lived
altruistic individuals again behaves like a single infinitely lived agent.

Two things should be said about what does and does not survive.  The objective remains
\emph{time-consistent}: viewed from date $t$, the dynasty maximizes $\sum_{s\ge t}\beta^{s-t}u_s(c_s)$,
which ranks continuation plans exactly as $\sum_{s\ge0}\beta^su_s(c_s)$ does, because the two differ by the
positive factor $\beta^{t}$ and by terms that are already sunk.  Geometric discounting, not the
identity of the period utility functions, is what delivers consistency (compare \bk{Exercise 5.1}).  What is
lost is \emph{stationarity}: the maximization problem is no longer autonomous, so the stationary dynamic
programming of Chapter 6 does not apply directly and one needs its nonstationary version (Section 6.7),
with value functions indexed by time.  Balanced growth also imposes a restriction: if
$u_t(c)=\gamma^tu(c)$ for a constant $\gamma>0$ --- taste change at a constant rate --- the objective is
$\sum(\beta\gamma)^tu(c_t)$ and stationarity is restored with the modified discount factor $\beta\gamma$,
which must satisfy $\beta\gamma<1$.  For general sequences $\{u_t\}$ neither a steady state nor a balanced
growth path need exist.

\emph{(b) Caring about grandchildren directly.}  Now
\[
V_t=u(c_t)+\beta V_{t+1}+\delta V_{t+2},
\qquad \beta,\delta>0 .
\]
Expanding, the weight $a_s$ that generation $t$ attaches to $u(c_{t+s})$ obeys the linear recursion
\[
a_0=1,\qquad a_1=\beta,\qquad a_s=\beta a_{s-1}+\delta a_{s-2}\quad(s\ge2),
\]
whose solution is a combination of the powers of the roots of $x^2=\beta x+\delta$, the dominant one being
$\varphi=\bigl(\beta+\sqrt{\beta^2+4\delta}\bigr)/2$.  Three consequences follow.

First, the objective is still an infinite discounted sum, $V_t=\sum_{s\ge0}a_su(c_{t+s})$, and it converges
if and only if $\varphi<1$, which is equivalent to
\[
\beta+\delta<1 .
\]
So the extra altruism must be paid for out of the total weight on the future.

Second --- and this is the substantive difference --- the discounting is \emph{not} geometric.  The ratios
of successive weights are
\[
\frac{a_1}{a_0}=\beta,
\qquad
\frac{a_2}{a_1}=\beta+\frac{\delta}{\beta}>\beta ,
\]
and they keep changing (converging to $\varphi$ only in the limit).  A sequence of weights that falls at a
varying rate is exactly the structure of the hyperbolic discounting of \bk{Exercise 5.1}(b), and it has the
same consequence: the plan that generation $t$ would like its descendants to follow is not the plan that
generation $t+1$ chooses, because generation $t$ discounts dates $t+1$ and $t+2$ in the ratio $\beta$ to
$\beta^2+\delta$, while generation $t+1$ discounts the same two dates in the ratio $1$ to $\beta$.
Preferences are \emph{time-inconsistent}, and the dynasty can no longer be represented as a single
maximizing agent.

Third, the modeling consequence: the right solution concept is not a maximization problem but an
intergenerational \emph{game}, in which each generation takes the decision rules of its descendants as
given; the natural equilibrium notion is a Markov-perfect (or ``consistent-planning'') equilibrium, and
saving behavior in it generally differs from the commitment solution --- the dynasty saves less than
generation $t$ would wish, since it cannot bind its descendant to treat the grandchild as generously as it
would like.  The economics is the same as in the individual hyperbolic-discounting model: an extra,
non-geometric concern for a more distant date creates a conflict between successive decision makers.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.1}{}
Following \bk{Example 6.1}, the state is $x=k$, the control is next period's capital $y=k'$, and
\[
U(k,k')=u\bigl(g(k)-k'\bigr),
\qquad
g(k)\equiv f(k)+(1-\delta)k,
\qquad
G(k)=\bigl[0,\,g(k)\bigr],
\]
the constraint $k'\le g(k)$ being $c\ge0$.  Assumptions 1 and 2 of Chapter 2 restrict $f$ only, so
throughout we also use the standard assumptions on the utility function collected in Assumption 3$'$: $u$
is continuous, strictly increasing, strictly concave, and (for \bk{Assumption 6.5}) continuously
differentiable.

\emph{A compact state space.}  First note that the state can be confined to a compact interval.  By
Assumption 2, $\lim_{k\to\infty}f(k)/k=0$, so there is a unique $\bar k>0$ with $f(\bar k)=\delta\bar k$:
at $\bar k$ the whole of output is needed to replace depreciated capital, and no feasible plan starting
below $\bar k$ can ever exceed it.  Hence, with
$\vec k\equiv g\bigl(\max\{k(0),\bar k\}\bigr)$, every feasible plan from $k(0)$ remains in
$X\equiv[0,\vec k]$, a compact set.

\emph{\bk{Assumption 6.1}.}  $G(k)\ni0$ is nonempty for every $k\in X$.  Since $X$ is compact, $g$ is
continuous and $u$ is continuous, $U$ is bounded on $X_G$ (a continuous function on a compact set), say
$|U|\le\bar U<\infty$.  Then for any feasible plan $\sum_{t=0}^{n}\beta^{t}U(k(t),k(t+1))$ is a Cauchy
sequence, since the tail is bounded by $\bar U\beta^{n+1}/(1-\beta)$; the limit exists and is finite.

\emph{\bk{Assumption 6.2}.}  $X$ is a compact subset of $\R$.  $G(k)=[0,g(k)]$ is nonempty- and
compact-valued, and it is continuous: both endpoints are continuous functions of $k$, which gives upper and
lower hemicontinuity.  $U$ is continuous on $X_G=\{(k,k'):0\le k'\le g(k)\}$ as the composition of the
continuous functions $u$ and $(k,k')\mapsto g(k)-k'$.

\emph{\bk{Assumption 6.3}.}  Take $(k,k')$, $(\tilde k,\tilde k')\in X_G$ and $\alpha\in(0,1)$.  Since $f$
is concave (Assumption 1), so is $g$, and since $u$ is increasing and concave,
\begin{align*}
U\bigl(\alpha k+(1-\alpha)\tilde k,\ \alpha k'+(1-\alpha)\tilde k'\bigr)
&=u\bigl(g(\alpha k+(1-\alpha)\tilde k)-\alpha k'-(1-\alpha)\tilde k'\bigr)\\
&\ge u\bigl(\alpha[g(k)-k']+(1-\alpha)[g(\tilde k)-\tilde k']\bigr)\\
&\ge\alpha U(k,k')+(1-\alpha)U(\tilde k,\tilde k') ,
\end{align*}
the first inequality by concavity of $g$ and monotonicity of $u$, the second by concavity of $u$.  If
$k\neq\tilde k$ the first inequality is strict, because $f$ is strictly concave (Assumption 1 has
$f''<0$) and $u$ is strictly increasing --- which is exactly the strict inequality required by
\bk{Assumption 6.3}.  Convexity of $G$ is the same computation: if $k'\le g(k)$ and $\tilde k'\le g(\tilde
k)$ then $\alpha k'+(1-\alpha)\tilde k'\le\alpha g(k)+(1-\alpha)g(\tilde k)\le g(\alpha k+(1-\alpha)\tilde
k)$.

\emph{\bk{Assumption 6.4}.}  $U(\cdot,k')$ is strictly increasing because
$\partial U/\partial k=u'(c)g'(k)=u'(c)[f'(k)+1-\delta]>0$, using $u'>0$ and $f'>0$ (Assumption 1).  And
$g$ is increasing, so $k\le\tilde k$ implies $G(k)=[0,g(k)]\subset[0,g(\tilde k)]=G(\tilde k)$: a larger
capital stock relaxes the constraint set.

\emph{\bk{Assumption 6.5}.}  On the interior of $X_G$ --- where $c=g(k)-k'>0$ --- $U$ is continuously
differentiable, since $u$ is continuously differentiable and $f$ is twice differentiable by Assumption 1.

All five assumptions therefore hold, and \bk{Theorems 6.1--6.6} apply to the optimal growth model, which is
what licenses the recursive treatment in Sections 6.6 and 6.8.

\rmk{Only two properties of the technology did real work: concavity of $f$ (for \bk{Assumption 6.3}) and
the Inada condition $f(k)/k\to0$ (for compactness of $X$).  The second is indispensable in this form of the
argument: an $AK$ technology, which violates Assumption 2, has an unbounded state space, and the
contraction-based theory of this chapter does not apply directly --- which is why the book turns to
continuous-time methods for endogenous growth models.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.4}{}
Recall the setting: $\dt x(t)=f(x(t))$ with $x(0)=c$, where $f$ is Lipschitz with constant $M$, and for
$g\in C[0,s]$,
\[
Tg(z)=c+\int_0^zf(g(x))\dd x ,\qquad z\in[0,s].
\]

\emph{(a) $T$ maps $C[0,s]$ into itself, and its fixed points are the solutions.}  If $g$ is continuous then
$f\circ g$ is continuous, so the integral is well defined and $Tg$ is continuously differentiable, hence
continuous: $T:C[0,s]\to C[0,s]$.  If $Tg=g$ then $g(z)=c+\int_0^zf(g(x))\dd x$, so $g(0)=c$ and, by the
Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, $g$ is differentiable with $\dt g(z)=f(g(z))$: $g$ solves \bk{(6.7)}.
Conversely, integrating \bk{(6.7)} shows every continuous solution is a fixed point.  For the contraction
property, Lipschitz continuity gives, for every $z\in[0,s]$,
\[
\bigl|Tg(z)-T\tilde g(z)\bigr|
\le\int_0^z\bigl|f(g(x))-f(\tilde g(x))\bigr|\dd x
\le M\int_0^z\bigl|g(x)-\tilde g(x)\bigr|\dd x
\le Mz\|g-\tilde g\| ,
\tag{$*$}
\]
so $\|Tg-T\tilde g\|\le Ms\|g-\tilde g\|$.  For $s<1/M$ this is a contraction, and since $C[0,s]$ with the
sup norm is complete, \bk{Theorem 6.7} gives a unique fixed point: \bk{(6.7)} has exactly one continuous
solution on $[0,s]$ when $s<1/M$.

\emph{(b) The second iterate.}  The useful form of ($*$) is the \emph{pointwise} bound
$|Tg(z)-T\tilde g(z)|\le Mz\|g-\tilde g\|$.  Applying $T$ again and using it inside the integral,
\[
\bigl|T^2g(z)-T^2\tilde g(z)\bigr|
\le M\int_0^z\bigl|Tg(x)-T\tilde g(x)\bigr|\dd x
\le M\int_0^zMx\|g-\tilde g\|\dd x
=\frac{M^2z^2}{2}\|g-\tilde g\| ,
\]
and taking the supremum over $z\in[0,s]$ gives $\|T^2g-T^2\tilde g\|\le M^2\frac{s^2}{2}\|g-\tilde g\|$.

\emph{(c) Induction.}  Suppose $|T^{n}g(z)-T^{n}\tilde g(z)|\le M^{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\|g-\tilde g\|$ for
all $z\in[0,s]$, which is ($*$) for $n=1$.  Then
\[
\bigl|T^{n+1}g(z)-T^{n+1}\tilde g(z)\bigr|
\le M\int_0^z\frac{M^{n}x^{n}}{n!}\|g-\tilde g\|\dd x
=\frac{M^{n+1}z^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\|g-\tilde g\| ,
\]
completing the induction; the sup over $[0,s]$ gives the stated bound.

\emph{(d) Any interval.}  Fix $s\in\R_+$ arbitrary.  Since $M^{n}s^{n}/n!\to0$ as $n\to\infty$ --- the
terms of the convergent series for $e^{Ms}$ --- there is $n$ with $M^{n}s^{n}/n!<1$, so $T^{n}$ is a
contraction on the complete metric space $C[0,s]$.  By \bk{Exercise 6.2}, if some iterate $T^{n}$ is a
contraction then $T$ itself has a unique fixed point.  Hence \bk{(6.7)} has a unique continuous solution on
$[0,s]$, for \emph{every} $s$, not merely for $s<1/M$.

\rmk{This is the Picard--Lindel\"of theorem, proved by contraction.  The restriction $s<1/M$ in
\bk{Example 6.3} is an artifact of using $T$ rather than its iterates: the factorial in part (c) beats the
geometric growth of $M^{n}s^{n}$, so the solution never breaks down at a finite horizon.  The same device ---
show that some iterate is a contraction even when the map itself is not --- is what makes the contraction
method usable in dynamic programming problems where the one-step operator is not a contraction, for instance
with $\beta=1$ but a bounded number of periods of ``effective'' discounting.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.5}{}
The hypotheses are that $\varphi:\R\times[a,b]\to\R$ is continuously differentiable with
$0<m\le\partial\varphi(y,x)/\partial y\le M<\infty$ for all $(y,x)$.

\emph{(a) $T$ is a contraction.}  For $y,\tilde y$ and any $x\in[a,b]$, the Mean Value Theorem gives a
$\xi$ between $y(x)$ and $\tilde y(x)$ with
$\varphi(y(x),x)-\varphi(\tilde y(x),x)=\varphi_y(\xi,x)\bigl[y(x)-\tilde y(x)\bigr]$.  Therefore
\[
Ty(x)-T\tilde y(x)
=\bigl[y(x)-\tilde y(x)\bigr]\Bigl[1-\frac{\varphi_y(\xi,x)}{M}\Bigr] ,
\]
and since $m\le\varphi_y\le M$ we have $0\le1-\varphi_y/M\le1-m/M$.  Hence
\[
\|Ty-T\tilde y\|\le\Bigl(1-\frac{m}{M}\Bigr)\|y-\tilde y\| ,
\qquad
\beta\equiv1-\frac{m}{M}\in[0,1),
\]
so $T$ is a contraction with modulus $\beta$.  That $T$ maps continuous functions to continuous functions
is immediate from continuity of $\varphi$.

\emph{(b) The theorem.}  $C([a,b])$ with the sup norm is a complete metric space, so by \bk{Theorem 6.7}
$T$ has a unique fixed point $y^*$.  By construction
\[
Ty^*=y^*
\iff
\frac{\varphi(y^*(x),x)}{M}=0
\iff
\varphi\bigl(y^*(x),x\bigr)=0
\quad\text{for all }x\in[a,b],
\]
which is the existence and uniqueness assertion of the Implicit Function Theorem.  Differentiability
follows once existence is in hand: since $\varphi$ is continuously differentiable and
$\varphi_y\ge m>0$, the identity $\varphi(y^*(x),x)=0$ can be differentiated to give
\[
\frac{\dd y^*(x)}{\dd x}=-\frac{\varphi_x\bigl(y^*(x),x\bigr)}{\varphi_y\bigl(y^*(x),x\bigr)} ,
\]
which is continuous, so $y^*\in C^1([a,b])$.

\rmk{The exercise suggests working directly in $C^1([a,b])$.  That is the natural guess but the wrong
space: with the $C^1$ norm $\|y\|+\|\dt y\|$, $T$ need not be a contraction, because differentiating $Ty$
brings in $\varphi_x$ and the derivative $\dt y$ with a coefficient that is not bounded by $\beta$.  The
clean route is the one above --- contract on $C([a,b])$, where the sup-norm estimate is exactly the
one-dimensional bound $|1-\varphi_y/M|\le1-m/M$, and recover continuous differentiability afterwards from
the implicit relation.  The uniform bound $\varphi_y\ge m>0$ is what makes the modulus strictly less than
one, and it is the same hypothesis that makes the implicit function well defined globally on $[a,b]$ rather
than only locally.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.6}{}
Recall
\[
TV(x)=\max_{y\in G(x)}\bigl\{U(x,y)+\beta V(y)\bigr\},
\qquad V\in C(X).
\]
Under \bk{Assumption 6.2}, $X$ is compact, $G$ is nonempty- and compact-valued and continuous, and $U$ is
continuous; so the maximum is attained (Weierstrass) and $TV\in C(X)$ by Berge's Maximum Theorem.  Since
$X$ is compact, every $V\in C(X)$ is bounded, and $C(X)$ with the sup norm is a complete metric space.  We
verify Blackwell's two sufficient conditions (\bk{Theorem 6.9}).

\emph{Monotonicity.}  Let $V,W\in C(X)$ with $V(y)\le W(y)$ for all $y$.  For every $x$ and every
$y\in G(x)$,
\[
U(x,y)+\beta V(y)\le U(x,y)+\beta W(y)\le TW(x),
\]
the last step because $TW(x)$ is the maximum over $y\in G(x)$.  Taking the maximum of the left-hand side
over $y\in G(x)$ gives $TV(x)\le TW(x)$ for all $x$, that is $TV\le TW$.

\emph{Discounting.}  For a constant $c\ge0$,
\[
T(V+c)(x)=\max_{y\in G(x)}\bigl\{U(x,y)+\beta V(y)+\beta c\bigr\}
=\Bigl[\max_{y\in G(x)}\bigl\{U(x,y)+\beta V(y)\bigr\}\Bigr]+\beta c
=TV(x)+\beta c ,
\]
because the additive constant $\beta c$ does not affect which $y$ attains the maximum.  So
$T(V+c)\le TV+\beta c$, with $\beta\in[0,1)$ the discount factor.

Both of Blackwell's conditions hold, so by \bk{Theorem 6.9} $T$ is a contraction on $C(X)$ with modulus
$\beta$, and \bk{Theorem 6.7} then delivers the unique fixed point $V\in C(X)$ that solves
\bk{Problem 6.3}.

\rmk{The proof shows that discounting does all the work: the modulus of the contraction \emph{is} the
discount factor.  This is why $\beta<1$ is not a technical convenience but the substantive hypothesis of
the whole theory --- with $\beta=1$ the operator satisfies monotonicity but not discounting, the fixed
point need not be unique or even exist, and problems like \bk{Exercise 6.15} have no solution at all.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.10}{}
It is enough to exhibit one.  Take the log/Cobb--Douglas optimal growth model of \bk{Example 6.4} with full
depreciation:
\[
\max\ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t}\log c(t)
\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad
k(t+1)=k(t)^{\alpha}-c(t),\qquad k(0)>0 .
\]
The Euler equation \bk{(6.50)} here is
\[
\frac{1}{c(t)}=\beta\,\alpha k(t+1)^{\alpha-1}\frac{1}{c(t+1)} .
\tag{$*$}
\]

\emph{The Euler equations alone leave one degree of freedom.}  Let $u(t)\equiv c(t)/k(t)^{\alpha}$ be the
consumption share, so that $k(t+1)=(1-u(t))k(t)^{\alpha}$.  Substituting into ($*$) and simplifying, the
Euler equation is equivalent to the first-order difference equation
\[
u(t+1)=\frac{\alpha\beta\,u(t)}{1-u(t)} ,
\]
with fixed points $u^*=1-\alpha\beta$ --- the familiar optimal policy, $c=(1-\alpha\beta)k^{\alpha}$ --- and
$u=0$.  The derivative of the map at $u^*$ is $\alpha\beta/(1-u^*)^2=1/(\alpha\beta)>1$, so $u^*$ is
\emph{unstable} going forward, while $u=0$ is stable (derivative $\alpha\beta<1$).  Consequently, for any
$u(0)\neq u^*$ the resulting path satisfies \emph{every} Euler equation, and:
\begin{itemize}
\item if $u(0)>u^*$ the consumption share rises until it exceeds $1$ and capital would have to be
      negative: the plan is infeasible after finitely many periods;
\item if $u(0)<u^*$ the path is feasible forever, but $u(t)\to0$ geometrically --- the economy saves an
      ever larger fraction of output and consumption vanishes relative to output.
\end{itemize}
The second family is the answer: these plans are feasible, satisfy all the Euler equations, and are
strictly suboptimal.

\emph{They fail transversality.}  The transversality condition \bk{(6.51)} here is
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta^{t}f'(k(t))u'(c(t))k(t)
=\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta^{t}\frac{\alpha k(t)^{\alpha}}{c(t)}
=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\alpha\beta^{t}}{u(t)}=0 .
\]
On the optimal path $u(t)\equiv u^*$ and the expression is $\alpha\beta^{t}/u^*\to0$.  On an over-saving
path $u(t+1)\approx\alpha\beta u(t)$ once $u$ is small, so $u(t)\sim C(\alpha\beta)^{t}$ and
\[
\frac{\alpha\beta^{t}}{u(t)}\sim\frac{\alpha}{C}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigr)^{t}\longrightarrow\infty ,
\]
so transversality fails as badly as possible.

\emph{And they are strictly worse.}  With $\alpha=1/3$, $\beta=0.9$ and $k(0)=1$, so that $u^*=0.7$:

The economics is transparent: the household keeps accumulating capital it never consumes.  Each Euler
equation is a statement about a \emph{local, one-period} deviation --- shifting a little consumption between
$t$ and $t+1$ cannot improve matters --- and every such deviation is indeed unprofitable along these paths.
What they miss is the \emph{global} deviation of consuming a fraction of the terminal capital stock, which
is what the transversality condition prices: when
$\lim\beta^{t}D_xU(x^*(t),x^*(t+1))\cdot x^*(t+1)>0$, the plan leaves asymptotically valuable capital
unconsumed, and shrinking the whole capital path by a factor $1-\varepsilon$ raises utility.  That is
exactly the perturbation used in the proof of \bk{Theorem 6.10}.

\rmk{The instability of the Euler map is not a defect of the example but the general situation: the Euler
equations form a second-order difference equation in the state, whose steady state is a saddle, so the
initial condition $k(0)$ pins down only one of the two degrees of freedom and transversality selects the
saddle path.  It also means that one cannot compute the optimum by iterating the Euler equation forward ---
even starting exactly at $u^*$, rounding error grows by a factor $1/(\alpha\beta)$ per period.  The
accompanying program therefore imposes the optimal path exactly rather than iterating it.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.11}{}
\emph{Shifting consumption up is always affordable.}  The only constraint is the flow budget constraint
\[
a(t+1)=(1+r(t))a(t)+w(t)-c(t),
\qquad a(0)\ \text{given}.
\]
Given a path $\{c(t)\}$ with assets $\{a(t)\}$, define $c'(t)\equiv c(t)+\gamma$ and let $\{a'(t)\}$ be
generated by the same flow constraint from the same $a(0)$.  The difference
$d(t)\equiv a'(t)-a(t)$ satisfies $d(0)=0$ and $d(t+1)=(1+r(t))d(t)-\gamma$, so
\[
d(t)=-\gamma\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\ \prod_{j=s+1}^{t-1}\bigl(1+r(j)\bigr)\ <0 .
\]
Every equation of the flow constraint holds by construction, so $\{c'(t)\}$ is feasible: the extra
consumption is simply borrowed, and the debt is rolled over forever.  With $r(t)\ge0$ the debt grows at
least linearly, and $a'(t)\to-\infty$.

\emph{Hence no optimum exists.}  Since $u$ is strictly increasing,
\[
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t}u\bigl(c(t)+\gamma\bigr)>\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t}u\bigl(c(t)\bigr)
\qquad\text{for every }\gamma>0 ,
\]
so no feasible path can be optimal: whatever is proposed, adding $\gamma$ to consumption at every date is
also feasible and strictly better.  If in addition $u$ is unbounded above, letting $\gamma\to\infty$ makes
the supremum $+\infty$; if $u$ is bounded above by $\bar u$, the supremum is $\bar u/(1-\beta)$ and is still
not attained.  Either way the maximization problem is not well posed --- \bk{Assumption 6.1} fails, and with
it the entire dynamic programming apparatus.

\emph{Why market economies exclude these paths.}  The path finances consumption by borrowing and never
repaying: the present value of the consumer's debt does not go to zero, so in present-value terms he is
consuming resources he never earns.  Nobody would take the other side of that trade.  Formally, the lender's
own optimization imposes that the value of the loan be recovered, so equilibrium requires a
\emph{no-Ponzi-game} condition
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}\ \frac{a(t)}{\prod_{j=0}^{t-1}(1+r(j))}\ \ge\ 0 ,
\]
or, equivalently and more primitively, the \emph{natural debt limit} \bk{(6.44)}: the consumer can borrow at
most the present value of his future labor income, since that is all he can ever repay with nonnegative
consumption.  Either restriction restores a compact feasible set and a well-posed problem, and in general
equilibrium the restriction is not an extra assumption at all --- aggregate assets must equal the aggregate
capital stock, which is finite, so debts must be backed by someone's assets.

\rmk{This is the discrete-time counterpart of the transversality condition of \bk{Exercise 6.10}, seen from
the household's side.  There the danger was accumulating capital forever and never consuming it; here it is
borrowing forever and never repaying.  Both are ruled out by the same kind of terminal condition, and both
show that the Euler equations alone do not characterize optimal behavior in infinite horizon.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.13}{}
Write $q(t)\equiv\prod_{j=0}^{t-1}\bigl(1+r(j)\bigr)^{-1}$ for the price at date $0$ of a unit of the good
at date $t$ (with $q(0)=1$), and let
\[
W(t)\equiv\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\Bigl(\prod_{j=0}^{s}\frac{1}{1+r(t+j)}\Bigr)w(t+s)
\]
be the present value at $t$ of current and future labor income, so that $\bar W=\max_tW(t)$.

\emph{(a) The natural debt limit.}  Iterating the flow budget constraint forward from $t$ and dividing by
the accumulated interest factors,
\[
\sum_{s=0}^{T}\Bigl(\prod_{j=0}^{s}\frac{1}{1+r(t+j)}\Bigr)c(t+s)
= a(t)+\sum_{s=0}^{T}\Bigl(\prod_{j=0}^{s}\frac{1}{1+r(t+j)}\Bigr)w(t+s)
-\Bigl(\prod_{j=0}^{T}\frac{1}{1+r(t+j)}\Bigr)a(t+T+1).
\]
The debt is repayable only if the household can make the left-hand side nonnegative for every $T$ while
letting the last term vanish.  Since consumption cannot be negative, the most the household can ever
dedicate to repayment is its entire future labor income; taking $T\to\infty$ and imposing $c\ge0$ gives
\[
a(t)\ \ge\ -\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\Bigl(\prod_{j=0}^{s}\frac{1}{1+r(t+j)}\Bigr)w(t+s)=-W(t),
\]
which is \bk{(6.44)}.  It binds exactly when the household plans zero consumption forever, which is why it
is called \emph{natural}: it is implied by feasibility alone, not by any extra market restriction.

\emph{(b) Bounds on assets.}  The lower bound is immediate: $a(t)\ge-W(t)\ge-\bar W$.  For the upper bound,
work with total wealth $\Omega(t)\equiv a(t)+W(t)\ge0$, which evolves as
$\Omega(t+1)=(1+r(t))\bigl(\Omega(t)-c(t)\bigr)$.  So $\Omega$ is nonincreasing as soon as the household
consumes at least the interest on its wealth,
\[
c(t)\ \ge\ \frac{r(t)}{1+r(t)}\Omega(t) ,
\]
and a sufficient condition for that at the optimum is $\beta(1+r(t))\le1$ for all $t$: with the Euler
equation \bk{(6.45)}, $\beta(1+r(t+1))\le1$ makes consumption nonincreasing, which (given the intertemporal
budget constraint) requires consuming at least the annuity value of wealth.  Under that condition
$\Omega(t)\le\Omega(0)=a(0)+W(0)\le a(0)+\bar W$, and since $W(t)\ge0$,
\[
a(t)=\Omega(t)-W(t)\le a(0)+\bar W .
\]
So the conditions are: $\bar W<\infty$ (finite lifetime resources) and $\beta(1+r(t))\le1$ for all $t$ (the
household is at least as impatient as the market).  Both are needed --- \bk{Exercise 6.12}(b) shows that
with $r>\beta^{-1}-1$ assets grow without bound and no such $\bar a$ exists.

\emph{(c) The level of initial consumption.}  The Euler equation \bk{(6.45)} determines the whole path from
its first element: define recursively $c(t)=h_t\bigl(c(0)\bigr)$ by
$u'(c(t))=\beta(1+r(t+1))u'(c(t+1))$, so each $h_t$ is strictly increasing in $c(0)$ because $u'$ is
strictly decreasing.  The transversality condition --- equivalently the no-Ponzi condition holding with
equality, $\lim_{t\to\infty}q(t)a(t)=0$ --- turns the flow constraints into the single intertemporal budget
constraint
\[
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}q(t)\,h_t\bigl(c(0)\bigr)\ =\ a(0)+\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}q(t)w(t)\ \equiv\ \Omega(0),
\tag{$*$}
\]
which determines $c(0)$ implicitly.  The left-hand side is continuous and strictly increasing in $c(0)$
(from $0$ to $\infty$), so the solution exists and is unique.  Differentiating ($*$),
\[
\frac{\partial c(0)}{\partial a(0)}=\Bigl[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}q(t)h_t'\bigl(c(0)\bigr)\Bigr]^{-1}>0 :
\]
an increase in initial assets raises initial consumption.  With CRRA utility
$h_t(c(0))=c(0)\prod_{j=1}^{t}\bigl[\beta(1+r(j))\bigr]^{1/\theta}$, so
$c(0)=\Omega(0)/\sum_tq(t)\prod_{j\le t}[\beta(1+r(j))]^{1/\theta}$ and the derivative is the marginal
propensity to consume out of wealth, a number in $(0,1]$.

\emph{(d) Retiming earnings.}  Let $\{\tilde w(t)\}$ satisfy $\tilde w(t)>w(t)$ for $t<T$,
$\tilde w(t)\le w(t)$ for $t\ge T$, and $\sum_t(1+r)^{-t}w(t)=\sum_t(1+r)^{-t}\tilde w(t)$.  Then
$\Omega(0)$ is unchanged, so by ($*$) the whole consumption path
$\{c(t)\}$ --- not merely $c(0)$ --- is unchanged.  What changes is the asset path: with income arriving
earlier, the household saves the difference, so
\[
\tilde a(t)-a(t)=\sum_{s<t}(1+r)^{t-s}\bigl[\tilde w(s)-w(s)\bigr]>0
\quad\text{for }0<t\le T,
\]
and the extra assets are run down thereafter, reaching zero in present value again.

The intuition is the permanent income hypothesis in its purest form.  With perfect capital markets, a
complete set of borrowing and lending opportunities at the market interest rate, and no binding constraint,
the household cares only about the \emph{present value} of its earnings; the timing affects only the
portfolio decision, not consumption.  Two qualifications are worth noting, because they are where the
result breaks in applications: if the natural debt limit \bk{(6.44)} or a tighter borrowing constraint
binds, a shift of income toward the present relaxes it and does raise consumption; and if the interest rate
at which the household can borrow exceeds that at which it can lend, the equivalence fails as well.  Here
neither applies --- the change makes the household unambiguously less constrained early on.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.14}{}
\emph{Conditions.}  The natural hypotheses are the stationary ones imposed \emph{uniformly in $t$}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $X\subset\R^K$ is compact, and for every $t$, $G(t,\cdot)$ is nonempty-valued, compact-valued and
      continuous, with the graph $X_G(t)=\{(x,y):y\in G(t,x)\}$ closed;
\item $U(t,\cdot,\cdot)$ is continuous on $X_G(t)$ for each $t$, and \emph{uniformly bounded}, in the sense
      that $\bar U\equiv\sup_{t}\sup_{(x,y)\in X_G(t)}|U(t,x,y)|$ is finite;
\item $\beta\in[0,1)$.
\end{enumerate}
Let $C(\Z_+\times X)$ be the space of functions $V(t,x)$, continuous in $x$ for each $t$ and bounded, with
the sup norm $\|V\|=\sup_{t,x}|V(t,x)|$.  This is a complete metric space.

\emph{$T$ is a well-defined self-map.}  For each $(t,x)$ the maximand is continuous in $y$ on the compact
set $G(t,x)$, so the maximum is attained (Weierstrass); by Berge's Maximum Theorem $TV(t,\cdot)$ is
continuous; and $\|TV\|\le\bar U+\beta\|V\|<\infty$, so $TV\in C(\Z_+\times X)$.  Uniform boundedness of
$U$ is exactly what is needed here --- without it the sup norm over all $t$ is infinite and the space is not
the right one.

\emph{$T$ is a contraction.}  Blackwell's conditions hold verbatim as in Exercise 6.6, because $t$ is
carried along as a parameter: if $V\le W$ pointwise then $TV\le TW$; and
$T(V+c)(t,x)=TV(t,x)+\beta c$ for any constant $c$.  By \bk{Theorem 6.9}, $T$ is a contraction of modulus
$\beta$, so by \bk{Theorem 6.7} it has a unique fixed point $V\in C(\Z_+\times X)$, the value function of
the nonstationary problem.

\emph{The equivalents of \bk{Theorems 6.2--6.6}.}  Write $\Phi(t,x)$ for the set of feasible plans from
state $x$ at date $t$, and $V^*(t,x)$ for the supremum of $\sum_{s\ge t}\beta^{s-t}U(s,x(s),x(s+1))$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Principle of optimality (6.2).}  $V^*(t,x^*(t))=U(t,x^*(t),x^*(t+1))+\beta V^*(t+1,x^*(t+1))$
      for an optimal plan, and any feasible plan satisfying this relation at every $t$ attains
      $V^*(t,x)$.  The proof is the stationary one with the date carried along: truncate the plan at $n$,
      use uniform boundedness to control the tail by $\bar U\beta^{n+1}/(1-\beta)$, and pass to the limit.
\item \emph{Existence (6.3).}  The fixed point $V$ exists and is unique by the contraction argument above,
      and $V=V^*$; the maximum in the Bellman equation is attained, which defines a (possibly
      set-valued) optimal policy $\pi(t,x)$.  Equivalently one can argue directly on the sequence problem:
      $\Phi(t,x)$ is compact in the product topology (Tychonoff), the objective is continuous, and
      Weierstrass applies.
\item \emph{Concavity (6.4).}  If in addition $U(t,\cdot,\cdot)$ is concave and $G(t,\cdot)$ has convex
      graph for each $t$, then $V(t,\cdot)$ is concave for each $t$.  Proof: the set of $V$ that are
      concave in $x$ for every $t$ is a closed subset of $C(\Z_+\times X)$, and $T$ maps it into the
      (smaller) set of functions strictly concave in $x$ when $U$ is strictly concave; apply
      \bk{Theorem 6.8}(2).
\item \emph{Monotonicity (6.5).}  If $U(t,\cdot,y)$ is strictly increasing and $G(t,\cdot)$ is monotone for
      each $t$, the same closed-subset argument gives $V(t,\cdot)$ strictly increasing.
\item \emph{Differentiability (6.6).}  If $U(t,\cdot,\cdot)$ is continuously differentiable and the optimal
      policy is interior, $x\in\operatorname{Int}X$ and $\pi(t,x)\in\operatorname{Int}G(t,x)$, then
      $V(t,\cdot)$ is differentiable at $x$ with $D_xV(t,x)=D_xU(t,x,\pi(t,x))$.  The Benveniste--Scheinkman
      argument is unchanged: $W(z)\equiv U(t,z,\pi(t,x))+\beta V(t+1,\pi(t,x))$ is differentiable, lies
      below $V(t,\cdot)$, and touches it at $x$.
\end{itemize}

\rmk{The one substantive difference from the stationary theory is uniformity.  Continuity and boundedness
must hold uniformly in $t$, since otherwise $C(\Z_+\times X)$ with the sup norm is not the relevant space
and the contraction modulus is not $\beta$.  Economically this rules out problems in which the environment
deteriorates or improves without bound --- for instance a wage sequence that diverges, which is precisely
why \bk{Example 6.5} needs $\bar W<\infty$.  The book's own treatment in Section 6.7 proves the analogous
results by working directly with the sequence problem; the contraction route above is shorter but buys that
brevity with the uniform bounds.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.15}{}
Note two features: there is no discounting ($\beta=1$) and no production, so the problem is to divide a
fixed cake $k(0)$ among infinitely many dates.  Feasibility requires $c(t)\ge0$ and
$\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}c(t)\le k(0)$.  Normalize $u(0)=0$; this is without loss, since if $u(0)>0$ then every plan that
consumes nothing in infinitely many periods already has value $+\infty$ and the problem is ill posed for a
still cruder reason, while if $u(0)<0$ the same is true with $-\infty$.

\emph{The supremum is not attained.}  Consider the plans
\[
c_n(t)=\begin{cases}k(0)/n,&t=0,\dots,n-1,\\ 0,&t\ge n,\end{cases}
\]
which are feasible and give value $V_n=n\,u\bigl(k(0)/n\bigr)$.  Because $u$ is strictly concave with
$u(0)=0$, the ratio $u(c)/c$ is strictly decreasing in $c$, so
\[
V_n=k(0)\cdot\frac{u(k(0)/n)}{k(0)/n}
\]
is strictly increasing in $n$, with
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}V_n=k(0)\cdot\lim_{c\downarrow0}\frac{u(c)}{c}=k(0)\,u'(0^+)\in(0,\infty] .
\]
On the other hand every feasible plan satisfies, again by strict concavity and $u(0)=0$,
\[
\sum_{t}u(c(t))<u'(0^+)\sum_{t}c(t)\le u'(0^+)k(0)
\]
whenever $u'(0^+)<\infty$ and some $c(t)>0$.  So the supremum equals $u'(0^+)k(0)$ and is approached by the
sequence $\{c_n\}$ but attained by no feasible plan: any candidate can be improved by splitting its
consumption into more, smaller pieces.  If $u'(0^+)=\infty$ the same computation shows the supremum is
$+\infty$, and again no plan attains it.  In both cases there is no solution.

\emph{Why.}  Without discounting there is no cost to postponing consumption, while strict concavity means
that the \emph{average} utility per unit consumed, $u(c)/c$, rises as the portions shrink.  Spreading the
cake ever more thinly over ever more dates therefore always raises total utility, and the ``optimum'' would
be to consume infinitesimal amounts forever --- which is not a feasible plan.  In the language of the
chapter, \bk{Assumption 6.1} fails: with $\beta=1$ the limit
$\lim_n\sum_{t=0}^{n}U(x(t),x(t+1))$ need not behave well, the objective is not continuous in the product
topology (the tail of a plan matters in the limit), and the Bellman operator satisfies Blackwell's
monotonicity but not the discounting condition of Exercise 6.6, so it is not a contraction.  Boundedness of
$u$ does not rescue matters --- it makes the supremum finite when $u'(0^+)<\infty$, but attainment is what
fails.

\rmk{The example isolates discounting as the substantive assumption.  Economists sometimes treat
$\beta<1$ as a mere technical device; here it is what makes the problem have a solution at all.  When
one does want to study undiscounted problems --- the golden rule, or the Ramsey problem with $\beta=1$ ---
the optimality criterion itself has to be weakened, to overtaking or catching-up criteria, as the footnote
to \bk{Assumption 6.1} notes.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.16}{}
\emph{(a) Recursive formulation.}  With $k$ the state and next period's capital $k'$ the control,
\[
V(k)=\max_{k'\in G(k)}\bigl\{u\bigl(f(k)-k'\bigr)+\beta V(k')\bigr\},
\qquad
G(k)=\bigl[0,f(k)\bigr] .
\]
As in Exercise 6.1, the state can be confined to the compact interval $[0,\vec k]$, where $\bar k$ solves

\emph{(b) Existence, uniqueness and shape.}  The problem satisfies \bk{Assumptions 6.1--6.4} by the
argument of Exercise 6.1, with $g(k)=f(k)$.  Hence:
\begin{itemize}
\item \bk{Theorem 6.3} gives a unique continuous bounded $V$ solving the Bellman equation, and an optimal
      plan exists;
\item \bk{Theorem 6.4} gives strict concavity of $V$, since $U(k,k')=u(f(k)-k')$ satisfies

\item \bk{Corollary 6.1} then gives a \emph{unique} optimal plan, generated by a continuous policy

\item \bk{Theorem 6.5} gives that $V$ is strictly increasing.

That $\pi$ is nondecreasing follows from the revealed-preference argument used for \bk{Proposition 6.1}:
if $k'>k$ but $\pi(k)>\pi(k')$, adding the two optimality inequalities gives
\[
u\bigl(z-\pi(k')\bigr)-u\bigl(z-\pi(k)\bigr)\le u\bigl(z'-\pi(k')\bigr)-u\bigl(z'-\pi(k)\bigr),
\qquad z\equiv f(k)<z'\equiv f(k'),
\]
which contradicts strict concavity of $u$, since the two consumption differences are equal in size but

\emph{(c) Differentiability.}  \bk{Theorem 6.6} gives differentiability of $V$ at any $k$ such that $u$ and

interior, $\pi(k)\in\operatorname{Int}G(k)$, that is $0<\pi(k)<f(k)$ --- equivalently both consumption and

V'(k)=\frac{\partial U(k,\pi(k))}{\partial k}=f'(k)\,u'\bigl(f(k)-\pi(k)\bigr).
\]
Without differentiability of $u$ (or at corner solutions) $V$ can have kinks, though it always has left and
right derivatives by concavity.

\emph{(d) The Euler equation.}  The first-order condition of the Bellman problem is
$u'(c)=\beta V'(k')$, and the envelope condition just derived is $V'(k)=f'(k)u'(c)$.  Combining, with
$c(t)=f(k(t))-k(t+1)$,
\[
\boxed{\ u'\bigl(c(t)\bigr)=\beta f'\bigl(k(t+1)\bigr)u'\bigl(c(t+1)\bigr)\ }
\]
the familiar condition that the marginal utility given up today equals the discounted marginal utility
gained tomorrow, multiplied by the marginal product of the capital carried forward.

\emph{(e) Is it enough?}  No.  Substituting $c(t)=f(k(t))-k(t+1)$, the Euler equation is a \emph{second
order} difference equation in $\{k(t)\}$,
\[
u'\bigl(f(k(t))-k(t+1)\bigr)=\beta f'\bigl(k(t+1)\bigr)u'\bigl(f(k(t+1))-k(t+2)\bigr),
\]
so the initial condition $k(0)$ leaves a one-parameter family of solutions, as \bk{Exercise 6.10} shows
explicitly.  The missing condition is the transversality condition
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta^{t}f'\bigl(k(t)\bigr)u'\bigl(c(t)\bigr)k(t)=0 ,
\]
which by \bk{Theorem 6.10} is necessary and sufficient, together with the Euler equations, for optimality of
an interior plan.  Intuitively it says that the present value of the capital stock left over at the end of
time must be zero: capital is worth holding only for the consumption it eventually delivers, so a plan that
keeps accumulating capital of positive present value forever is wasting resources.  It is the
infinite-horizon analogue of the terminal condition $k(T+1)=0$ in a finite-horizon problem --- one would
never end a finite life with capital left in the ground.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.17}{}
\emph{The consumption function is nondecreasing.}  By \bk{Proposition 6.1} the policy function $s(k)$ --- the
capital carried into the next period --- is nondecreasing, and by \bk{Proposition 6.2} the value function
$V$ is differentiable; it is strictly concave by \bk{Theorem 6.4}, so $V'$ is strictly decreasing.  The
first-order condition of the recursive problem \bk{(6.48)} is
\[
u'\bigl(c(k)\bigr)=\beta V'\bigl(s(k)\bigr) .
\]
Take $k'>k$.  Then $s(k')\ge s(k)$, so $V'(s(k'))\le V'(s(k))$, and therefore
\[
u'\bigl(c(k')\bigr)=\beta V'\bigl(s(k')\bigr)\le\beta V'\bigl(s(k)\bigr)=u'\bigl(c(k)\bigr).
\]
Since $u$ is strictly concave, $u'$ is strictly decreasing, and the inequality inverts:
$c(k')\ge c(k)$.  Hence $c(\cdot)$ is nondecreasing, as claimed.

\emph{Monotone convergence of consumption.}  Suppose $k(0)<k^*$.  By \bk{Proposition 6.3} the capital stock
increases monotonically to the steady state, $k(t)\uparrow k^*$.  Composing with the nondecreasing and
continuous function $c(\cdot)$,
\[
c(t)=c\bigl(k(t)\bigr)\ \text{is nondecreasing, and}\quad c(t)\to c(k^*)
\]
by continuity of $c(\cdot)$ (\bk{Corollary 6.1}).  The limit is the steady-state consumption level: at
$k^*$ the resource constraint with $k(t+1)=k(t)=k^*$ gives
\[
c^*=f(k^*)+(1-\delta)k^*-k^*=f(k^*)-\delta k^* .
\]
Thus $c(t)\uparrow c^*$.  If instead $k(0)>k^*$, \bk{Proposition 6.3} gives $k(t)\downarrow k^*$ and the
same argument gives $c(t)\downarrow c^*$.

\rmk{The result is worth contrasting with the Solow model.  There the saving rate is constant, so
consumption is a fixed fraction of output and inherits its monotonicity mechanically.  Here the saving rate
is chosen, and could in principle move in either direction; what pins the sign down is the combination of
two facts --- the economy accumulates capital monotonically, and the marginal value of capital falls as
capital rises.  Note also what is \emph{not} claimed: the saving \emph{rate} need not be monotone along the
transition, and generally is not.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.19}{}
Suppose, to get a contradiction, that there are arbitrarily small $\varepsilon>0$ with
$s(\varepsilon)\le\varepsilon$.  Along the optimal path starting from $k=\varepsilon$, the Euler equation
\bk{(6.50)} holds:
\[
u'\bigl(c(\varepsilon)\bigr)
=\beta\Bigl[f'\bigl(s(\varepsilon)\bigr)+1-\delta\Bigr]\,u'\Bigl(c\bigl(s(\varepsilon)\bigr)\Bigr).
\]
By \bk{Proposition 6.4} (Exercise 6.17) the consumption function is nondecreasing, so
$s(\varepsilon)\le\varepsilon$ implies $c(s(\varepsilon))\le c(\varepsilon)$ and hence
$u'\bigl(c(s(\varepsilon))\bigr)\ge u'\bigl(c(\varepsilon)\bigr)>0$, because $u'$ is decreasing.
Dividing the Euler equation by $u'(c(\varepsilon))$,
\[
1=\beta\Bigl[f'\bigl(s(\varepsilon)\bigr)+1-\delta\Bigr]
\frac{u'\bigl(c(s(\varepsilon))\bigr)}{u'\bigl(c(\varepsilon)\bigr)}
\ \ge\ \beta\Bigl[f'\bigl(s(\varepsilon)\bigr)+1-\delta\Bigr] ,
\]
that is
\[
f'\bigl(s(\varepsilon)\bigr)\ \le\ \frac{1}{\beta}-1+\delta\ <\ \infty .
\]
But $0<s(\varepsilon)\le\varepsilon$, so letting $\varepsilon\to0$ forces $s(\varepsilon)\to0$, and
Assumption 2 (the Inada condition $\lim_{k\to0}f'(k)=\infty$) gives
$f'(s(\varepsilon))\to\infty$ --- contradicting the bound just derived.  Hence for all sufficiently small
$\varepsilon$ we must have $s(\varepsilon)>\varepsilon$.

\emph{Intuition.}  Near zero the marginal product of capital is arbitrarily large, so an extra unit of
saving returns arbitrarily much tomorrow.  A household with a finite rate of time preference --- the
right-hand side $\beta^{-1}-1+\delta$ is a fixed, finite number, the effective required return --- will
always take that trade: at very low capital the return to accumulation exceeds the required return by an
unbounded margin.  Thus the economy cannot optimally run its capital stock down toward zero, and $k=0$,
though a rest point of the resource constraint, is never approached from a positive initial stock.  This is
precisely what the proof of \bk{Proposition 6.3} needs to conclude that the monotone sequence $\{k(t)\}$
converges to the \emph{positive} steady state $k^*$ rather than to zero.
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.20}{}
The claims of \bk{Proposition 6.1} are: there exist a unique value function $V$ and consumption function
$c(k)$, with $s(k)=f(k)+(1-\delta)k-c(k)$; $V$ is strictly increasing and strictly concave; and $s(k)$ is
nondecreasing.  Assume $u$ is continuous, strictly increasing and strictly concave, but \emph{not}
necessarily differentiable, and let $f$ satisfy Assumptions 1 and 2.

\emph{Step 1: the dynamic programming assumptions still hold.}  Exercise 6.1 verified
\bk{Assumptions 6.1--6.4} using only continuity, monotonicity and strict concavity of $u$ together with
concavity and monotonicity of $f$; differentiability entered only in \bk{Assumption 6.5}.  So
\bk{Assumptions 6.1--6.4} hold as before, while \bk{Assumption 6.5} is dropped.

\emph{Step 2: existence, uniqueness, concavity, monotonicity.}  \bk{Theorems 6.3, 6.4} and \bk{6.5} and
\bk{Corollary 6.1} require only \bk{Assumptions 6.1--6.4}.  They give, in order: a unique continuous
bounded $V$ solving the Bellman equation \bk{(6.48)}, with an optimal plan existing; $V$ strictly concave;
$V$ strictly increasing; and a unique optimal plan generated by a continuous policy function.  None of
these arguments differentiates anything: the contraction argument uses Blackwell's conditions (Exercise
6.6), and strict concavity is obtained from \bk{Theorem 6.8}(2), by showing that $T$ maps the closed set of
weakly concave functions into the set of strictly concave ones.

\emph{Step 3: $s(k)$ is nondecreasing.}  This is the only part of \bk{Proposition 6.1} whose proof might
seem to need calculus, and it does not: the book's argument is a revealed-preference argument.  Suppose
$k'>k$ but $s(k)>s(k')$.  Both $s(k)$ and $s(k')$ are feasible at both states, since
$f(k)+(1-\delta)k<f(k')+(1-\delta)k'$ and $s(k')<s(k)$.  Optimality at $k$ and at $k'$ gives
\begin{align*}
u\bigl(z-s(k)\bigr)+\beta V\bigl(s(k)\bigr)&\ge u\bigl(z-s(k')\bigr)+\beta V\bigl(s(k')\bigr),\\
u\bigl(z'-s(k')\bigr)+\beta V\bigl(s(k')\bigr)&\ge u\bigl(z'-s(k)\bigr)+\beta V\bigl(s(k)\bigr),
\end{align*}
with $z\equiv f(k)+(1-\delta)k<z'\equiv f(k')+(1-\delta)k'$.  Adding and rearranging eliminates $V$:
\[
u\bigl(z-s(k')\bigr)-u\bigl(z-s(k)\bigr)\ \le\ u\bigl(z'-s(k')\bigr)-u\bigl(z'-s(k)\bigr).
\]
But the two consumption increments are equal, $\bigl(z-s(k')\bigr)-\bigl(z-s(k)\bigr)=
\bigl(z'-s(k')\bigr)-\bigl(z'-s(k)\bigr)=s(k)-s(k')>0$, while the right-hand pair is evaluated at strictly
higher consumption levels ($z'>z$).  Strict concavity of $u$ makes equal increments worth strictly less at
higher levels, so the right-hand side is \emph{strictly smaller} than the left --- a contradiction.  Hence
$s(k)$ is nondecreasing.

This completes the proof of \bk{Proposition 6.1} using only continuity, monotonicity and strict concavity.

\rmk{What is genuinely lost without differentiability is \bk{Proposition 6.2} --- that $V$ is
differentiable --- and with it the Euler equation \bk{(6.50)} in its classical form.  Concavity still gives
$V$ left and right derivatives everywhere, and the first-order condition becomes the inclusion
$u'_{-}(c)\ge\beta V'_{+}(s)$ and $u'_{+}(c)\le\beta V'_{-}(s)$, or in the language of convex analysis,
$0\in\partial\bigl[u(z-s)+\beta V(s)\bigr]$ with $\partial$ the superdifferential.  All the qualitative
conclusions of Sections 6.8 --- uniqueness of the steady state, monotone convergence --- survive, because
they rest on the monotonicity of $s(\cdot)$ established above rather than on calculus.}
\end{aex}

\begin{aex}{6.21}{}
\emph{The two problems generate the same difference equations.}  In the competitive equilibrium, firms hire
capital and labor competitively, so
\[
R(t)=f'\bigl(k(t)\bigr),
\qquad
1+r(t+1)=f'\bigl(k(t)\bigr)+1-\delta,
\qquad
w(t)=f\bigl(k(t)\bigr)-k(t)f'\bigl(k(t)\bigr),
\]
and asset market clearing requires $a(t)=k(t)$.  Substituting the factor prices into the household's flow
budget constraint \bk{(6.54)},
\[
a(t+1)=(1+r(t))a(t)+w(t)-c(t)
=k(t)+\bigl[f'(k(t))-\delta\bigr]k(t)+f(k(t))-k(t)f'(k(t))-c(t),
\]
that is
\[
k(t+1)=f\bigl(k(t)\bigr)+(1-\delta)k(t)-c(t) ,
\]
which is exactly the resource constraint \bk{(6.47)} of the optimal growth problem.  Substituting the same
factor prices into the household's Euler equation \bk{(6.55)} gives \bk{(6.56)},
\[
u'\bigl(c(t)\bigr)=\beta\Bigl[f'\bigl(k(t+1)\bigr)+1-\delta\Bigr]u'\bigl(c(t+1)\bigr),
\]
which is identical to the planner's Euler equation \bk{(6.50)}.  The two problems also share the initial
condition $k(0)$, and the household's no-Ponzi condition with $a(t)=k(t)$,
$\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta^{t}u'(c(t))k(t+1)=0$, is the planner's transversality condition \bk{(6.51)}.

\emph{Uniqueness closes the argument.}  By \bk{Theorem 6.10}, for this problem the Euler equations together
with the transversality condition are necessary \emph{and} sufficient for optimality, and by
\bk{Corollary 6.1} --- the objective being strictly concave and the constraint set convex --- the optimal
plan is unique.  So there is exactly one sequence $\{k(t),c(t)\}$ satisfying (i) the resource constraint,
(ii) the Euler equation, (iii) $k(0)$ given, and (iv) transversality.  The competitive equilibrium path
satisfies all four, and so does the optimal growth path; therefore they coincide.

\emph{Both directions.}  The argument above takes an equilibrium and shows it solves the planner's problem.
The converse is equally immediate: given the optimal path $\{k(t),c(t)\}$, define prices by the marginal
product conditions above and set $a(t)=k(t)$.  Then firms maximize profits by construction (they take
prices as given and earn zero profits, by Euler's Theorem and constant returns), the household's budget
constraint holds at every date by the computation above, its Euler equation is the planner's, and its
transversality condition holds.  Hence the optimal allocation, supported by these prices, is a competitive
equilibrium.

\rmk{This is the First and Second Welfare Theorems of Chapter 5 in action, made concrete: the equivalence
holds because there are no externalities, no distortions and no missing markets, and because the natural
debt limit rules out the Ponzi schemes of \bk{Exercise 6.11}.  The practical payoff is large --- it is why
one may compute equilibria of the neoclassical growth model by solving a single planner's dynamic
programming problem, a device used throughout the rest of the book.  It is also worth noting where it
stops: as soon as Chapter 11 onward introduces externalities or monopolistic competition, the planner's and
the equilibrium paths separate, and this shortcut is no longer available.}
\end{aex}
